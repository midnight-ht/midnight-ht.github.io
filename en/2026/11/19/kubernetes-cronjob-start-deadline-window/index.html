Why can a CronJob skip a Job after a one-minute delay?

Compare a CronJob's scheduled time with its controller check to explain a missed occurrence under thirty- and ninety-second windows.

M Midnight Engineering notes on development, quality, security, delivery, and operations. View Author Profile
Updated 2026-11-19 · Estimated read 12 min
Translations
zh-CN en

Article

Key takeaways

  • Measure lateness from the planned occurrence.
  • Verify eligibility separately from execution and completion.
  • Preserve the deadline effective at the time of the event.

After a CronJob misses its planned creation time, startingDeadlineSeconds limits how late that occurrence may start. If the controller checks after the deadline, it skips the occurrence while future schedules continue. This field does not set the runtime of a container that has already started.

That explains a particular missing task. A job runs every minute, but the controller is unavailable for one occurrence. After recovery, that period still has no Job. A short lateness allowance does not promise replay of every old occurrence. Compare the planned time with the controller's check time, then inspect the window that applied.

Change only the lateness allowance

This is an unexecuted isolated-cluster exercise. Save this as short-window.yaml in a disposable namespace. The cluster must support these CronJob fields and the UTC timezone and be able to pull the image. The workload prints a marker once and has no business side effects.

apiVersion: batch/v1
kind: CronJob
metadata:
  name: short-window
spec:
  schedule: "* * * * *"
  timeZone: Etc/UTC
  startingDeadlineSeconds: 30
  jobTemplate:
    spec:
      template:
        spec:
          restartPolicy: Never
          containers:
          - name: marker
            image: busybox:1.36
            command: ["sh", "-c", "echo scheduled-attempt"]

Create a separate proposed definition named long-window with ninety seconds and otherwise equivalent settings. Record their identities independently. A real delayed-creation test needs a controlled controller state or reproducible environment. This article did not stop a controller, contact a cluster, or apply the manifest. The following numbers are hypothetical inputs to the documented rule.

Take 12:01:00 UTC as the planned time and 12:01:45 as the next controller check. Forty-five seconds exceeds short-window's thirty seconds, so that occurrence is skipped. Long-window permits ninety seconds, so this condition alone would not reject it. Move the check to 12:02:45 and the same occurrence is one hundred and five seconds late, beyond ninety too. Every comparison uses the same scheduled occurrence. The next minute does not reset the earlier occurrence's delay. CronJob delayed-start deadlines

Eligibility under this window is limited evidence. It does not guarantee immediate Job creation or a runnable Pod. Check concurrency policy, controller health, admission, and placement against the actual environment. A record that recovery or submission succeeded does not establish creation of the missing occurrence.

Preserve the identity of the missed period

Save the CronJob identity, schedule, timezone, and deadline effective at the time. Find the planned occurrence and its relevant controller records. If a Job exists, supported versions can supply a scheduled-time annotation. If it does not, use controller and scheduling evidence to investigate. Object absence cannot supply the planned timestamp, and the time someone opens a dashboard is not the time the controller checked.

Suppose a collector produces one batch per minute. A later successful batch does not prove the earlier gap was filled. Decide whether that period must be recovered, then give the recovery a clear input identity. Record when the recovery runs separately from the business period it covers. External side effects still need business deduplication and outcome evidence. The deadline only governs late creation eligibility.

Preserve the old setting and change time if an operator enlarges the window after a missed run. A read of today's configuration cannot explain yesterday's controller decision. Associate the event with its then-effective setting, then decide on compensation separately. Changing the configuration is not a recovery receipt. An old thirty-second window followed by a ninety-second change could otherwise make a legitimate earlier skip look inconsistent with policy. This configuration-evidence scenario was not run.

Decide whether a late result is still useful

A backup may tolerate hours of delay, while a minute-by-minute refresh can allow the next result to replace the previous one. Translate that requirement into the window, then verify a timeline clearly inside it and another clearly outside. Avoid exact-boundary examples when observation precision could obscure the comparison. Forty-five seconds against thirty and ninety gives distinguishable outcomes without equality at the cutoff.

A long-running started task supplies another control. Satisfying the creation deadline does not stop that task thirty seconds into execution. Runtime limits need the corresponding Job or container mechanism and its effective configuration. Combining late creation and long execution in one alert can send an operator to the wrong evidence. Job creation, Pod placement, and business completion each require their own observation.

Keep occurrences separate after crossing a minute boundary. At 12:02:10, a 12:01 occurrence is seventy seconds late and a 12:02 occurrence is ten seconds late. Neither exceeds a ninety-second window. This arithmetic does not promise two catch-up Jobs; actual creation still depends on the full policy and controller behavior. Associate a newly found Job with its planned occurrence before deciding whether the earlier gap remains. This is another hypothetical timeline.

Does a five-second window make the task more punctual?

It cannot promise that. The official documentation says the controller checks roughly every ten seconds, so a startingDeadlineSeconds value below ten can prevent scheduling. A smaller window narrows permitted lateness without increasing check frequency. If the business accepts only a few seconds of error, validate whether the scheduling mechanism meets that contract and define what happens when it does not. The proposed windows are thirty and ninety seconds. No five-second case or actual controller cadence was measured.
