Why can a GitHub Actions job time out before a step reaches its own limit?

Explain step and job budgets with a complete serial workflow, then inspect cancellation reasons, external task state, and report evidence.

M Midnight Engineering notes on development, quality, security, delivery, and operations. View Author Profile
Updated 2026-11-01 · Estimated read 13 min
Translations
zh-CN en

Article

Key takeaways

  • A step limit does not extend the shared job budget.
  • Distinguish platform cancellation from command failure.
  • Job termination does not resolve external task or upload outcomes.

A GitHub Actions job can exhaust its overall budget before a step reaches its own timeout-minutes. A step limit does not extend the job limit, and earlier serial work consumes the same job budget. Identify the layer that ended the run before changing a step, the total allowance, or the division of work.

The two settings bound different objects

The workflow syntax reference defines step and job timeout-minutes separately. A step limit bounds that step; a job limit bounds the job. Runner execution limits can end it earlier than the configured value. This article checked the current reference on November 1, 2026 and discusses ordinary serial steps. No hosted or self-hosted workflow was run.

The local allowance answers how long one step may run. The outer allowance answers how long the job may run. A second step does not receive a fresh outer budget. If earlier work consumed most of it, a generous second-step setting cannot require the platform to keep the whole job alive.

Distinguish cancellation from command failure too. A nonzero command exit, manual cancellation, job timeout, and runner failure can all leave later steps absent. Preserve the run result, reason, and last executed step before editing settings. A missing end marker does not identify a too-small step timeout.

A complete example without repository dependencies

This unexecuted workflow can be saved in an isolated test repository. It uses manual dispatch and two waits, whose steps use no checkout or third-party action, initiate no business network request, and deploy nothing. The runner still has its own platform communication. The numbers construct a comparison rather than prescribe a real pipeline budget.

name: inspect-timeout-boundaries
on: workflow_dispatch
permissions:
  contents: read
jobs:
  budget:
    runs-on: ubuntu-latest
    timeout-minutes: 2
    steps:
      - name: First bounded wait
        timeout-minutes: 2
        run: |
          printf 'first started\n'
          sleep 90
          printf 'first ended\n'
      - name: Second bounded wait
        timeout-minutes: 2
        run: |
          printf 'second started\n'
          sleep 90
          printf 'second ended\n'

Each planned wait is shorter than its two-minute step allowance. Together they exceed the job's two-minute budget. That explains how the second step can lose its execution opportunity before using its own limit. Actual handling and scheduling add conditions; this article promises no cancellation at precisely second 120 and contains no captured run log.

One comparison changes only the job allowance while retaining the work and step settings. Another shortens one step allowance to exercise a local timeout. Observe end markers and platform results separately. Increasing every value at once and succeeding does not locate the original constraint.

Markers also have limited meaning. Printing first ended shows that script location was reached, not completion of later work. An absent second ended has multiple possible causes. Correlate markers with the platform's terminal result rather than reconstructing the lifecycle from two strings.

How should the budget be allocated?

Inventory mandatory work, optional diagnostics, and necessary failure handling, then use actual duration observations. Variability, initialization, and data size affect the choice; no measured project distribution supports a universal multiplier here. The budget should express a requirement rather than always use the largest permitted number.

A frequently stuck step may need a repaired wait condition, dependency, or loop rather than a longer job. If individual steps finish reasonably but their serial total exceeds the outer allowance, evaluate the job budget or decomposition. Logs should explain where time went rather than treating every cancellation as a request to wait longer.

Additional jobs change budget boundaries but introduce output, artifact, and state handoffs. Preserve version identity and failure propagation. Work tied to one transaction or temporary environment may not divide cleanly; decide according to the real resource lifecycle.

Does stopping the job stop an external task?

A stopped runner does not establish another system's terminal state. A step might submit a remote build or batch task and then wait. Cancellation can remove the waiter without proving that the remote operation stopped. Query the external task identity, request cancellation where appropriate, and verify its result.

This affects retries. Repeating an unconfirmed submission can create a second operation. Retain necessary non-sensitive correlation information and use the existing interface to resolve the first attempt. Business idempotency needs its own contract. The fixture uses only local sleep and supplies no generic remote-cancellation API.

Step outcome and conclusion describes another distinction: permission to continue does not establish original success. Likewise, a successful diagnostic step cannot erase cancellation or missing evidence for mandatory work.

Does an always step guarantee report delivery?

A condition name cannot certify completed upload. Check whether execution remains possible, the file exists, the runner is available, and the upload has a successful result. Evidence produced only at the final instant may never exist after early termination.

Tools can write appropriate intermediate results and bind them to the run, commit, and check identity rather than reusing old files. Continuous external records or staged artifacts need access and retention review; this is not a suggestion to export all environment variables or secrets.

A proposed validation separates step timeout, job-budget expiry, manual cancellation, and absent reports, recording markers, platform reasons, artifact identity, and blocked downstream actions. Check actual runner limits as well as YAML values. These tests have not been executed.

A useful budget record names each local limit, the shared job boundary, earlier platform constraints, and ownership of unresolved work after termination. Those distinctions make an apparently premature final-step cancellation explainable without trial-and-error parameter increases.
