Stop when p drops below .05: what happened to the original guarantee?

A single-look cutoff does not automatically control stopping at any significant look. An exact two-look teaching example distinguishes fixed horizons, sequential inference and safety stops.

M Midnight Engineering notes on development, quality, security, delivery, and operations. View Author Profile
Updated 2026-11-08 · Estimated read 13 min
Translations
zh-CN en

Article

Key takeaways

  • Specify whether the guarantee covers one look or the stopping policy.
  • Cumulative looks are dependent; independent-test arithmetic is insufficient.
  • Record safety action separately from an error-controlled benefit claim.

A dashboard can be opened daily, but declaring a winner whenever an ordinary p-value crosses a cutoff requires a different statistical design. A 5% threshold at one time does not automatically control the decision to stop after any significant look. Changing the stopping rule changes the error event being controlled.

This does not require ignoring harm. The problem is using one improvised threshold for monitoring, safety action and proof of benefit while retaining a fixed-horizon claim. Section 1.1 of Ham and colleagues’ original confidence-sequence paper distinguishes pointwise coverage from coverage across time. This discussion identifies the source by v3 and the retrieved snapshot; an additional date rendered on the page is not treated as evidence of a new revision.

Which event does the threshold control?

A fixed-horizon test can control erroneous rejection at a prespecified sample size. If the team recalculates each day and stops at the first significant result, deployment is triggered by the union of those rejection events. Individually acceptable tests do not make that union obey the same bound. An early chance deviation can stop the experiment before later observations would have moderated it.

Cumulative daily observations overlap. An independent-tests formula with the number of days substituted is therefore not a generic calculation of the actual false-positive rate. Arrival patterns, delayed outcomes, the statistic and the stopping policy all matter. Validate the complete policy rather than just one p-value function.

The related product denominator discussion requires comparable business windows. There is an additional condition here: do not shorten a window after favorable results and extend it after unfavorable ones. A deadline written down and then selected according to the data is no longer the original plan.

Count all 4,096 paths

Consider a teaching model of independent fair binary observations. It is not a production analysis of two conversion groups. Under its null, each observation is zero or one with probability one half. Twelve observations produce 4,096 equally likely paths.

At the planned endpoint, use a two-sided exact binomial test: double the smaller inclusive tail probability and compare it with .05. At twelve observations, rejection occurs at two or fewer ones, or ten or more. Each tail contains 1 + 12 + 66 = 79 paths. The terminal test therefore rejects 158 paths, or approximately 3.857% of all paths. A discrete test need not use its entire nominal 5% allowance.

Now also look after six observations and stop if either look rejects. Six zeros or six ones rejects at the early look. Some such paths no longer reject at twelve. After six initial zeros, the remaining six observations can contain three, four, five or six ones: 20 + 15 + 6 + 1 possibilities. The symmetric case contributes the same number, adding 84 paths. The any-look event contains 242 paths, approximately 5.908%.

The following complete script has not been executed as this publication’s example. It supplies no runtime screenshot or real experiment output. The expected counts come independently from the combinatorial calculation above. Integer comparison keeps floating-point cutoff effects out of the illustration.

from itertools import product
from math import comb

def rejects(k, n):
    tail = sum(comb(n, j) for j in range(min(k, n-k) + 1))
    return 40 * tail <= 2 ** n

terminal = early_only = 0
for path in product((0, 1), repeat=12):
    at_end = rejects(sum(path), 12)
    terminal += at_end
    early_only += rejects(sum(path[:6]), 6) and not at_end
assert terminal == 158
assert early_only == 84
print({'all_paths': 4096, 'terminal': terminal,
       'any_look': terminal + early_only,
       'terminal_rate': terminal / 4096,
       'any_look_rate': (terminal + early_only) / 4096})

Forty comes from rearranging twice the tail probability being at most .05 into an integer inequality. This fixture counts one particular stopping policy. It neither estimates a company’s dashboard error rate nor predicts the same percentage for every early-stopping practice. It disproves the narrower claim that repeating a single-look cutoff automatically preserves an experiment-wide 5% bound.

Choose a stopping contract before declaring a winner

With fixed-horizon tooling, prespecify the randomization unit, primary metric, sample target, business duration and outcome-maturity requirement. Monitoring can continue, while the win decision follows the plan. A favorable day does not justify an unplanned early finish, and an unfavorable result does not justify collecting indefinitely until significance appears.

When early decisions are necessary, consider a group-sequential design with planned looks and boundaries, or an anytime-valid procedure under appropriate assumptions. The original paper expresses time-uniform coverage through confidence sequences and distinguishes exact and asymptotic constructions. Relabeling an ordinary interval is insufficient. Review allocation, estimands, dependence and the finite-sample promise of the implementation.

The choice depends on when the team must act and whether it can maintain the corresponding statistical procedure. Temporary metric changes, opportunistic segments and omitted unsuccessful experiments introduce further selection. A sequential tool does not resolve those issues by itself.

Must a harmful experiment run until its planned endpoint?

No. A safety policy can require immediate exposure shutdown. Retain the reason, authority and data available at that time. That risk decision need not masquerade as proof that another version produces a benefit. An error-controlled effect conclusion after stopping still needs inference compatible with the actual policy.

Discovery stop criteria also permit ending investment, but that product principle is not a sequential statistical guarantee. A team can accurately report “stopped for a harm signal; benefit unconfirmed.”

Proposed validation first checks the 158 endpoint paths and 84 additional paths, then evaluates the complete stopping policy under an explicit null model. A real experiment also needs look times, maturity state, metric versions and stopping reasons. A final significant screenshot alone cannot establish which contract was followed.
