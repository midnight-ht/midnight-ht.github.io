A pilot survey does not replace cognitive pretesting

Use five synthetic ledger entries to compare cognitive probes and a field pilot, separating complete answers from shared meaning and record availability.

M Midnight Engineering notes on development, quality, security, delivery, and operations. View Author Profile
Updated 2026-11-08 · Estimated read 15 min
Translations
zh-CN en

Article

Key takeaways

  • Completion and response distributions do not reconstruct interpretation on their own.
  • Cognitive sessions and field pilots can complement one another while retaining their distinct conditions.
  • Retrospective probes preserve the first answer but still face recall and rationalization limits.

A small pilot survey does not automatically replace cognitive pretesting. A pilot can reveal missing responses, routing problems and operational burden. A cognitive session investigates how respondents understand, retrieve and choose an answer. Complete submissions and tidy distributions can still combine answers about calendar months with answers about rolling periods. Choose the method according to the interpretation you need to investigate.

What a submission does not show

The US Census Bureau’s Appendix A2 on questionnaire testing distinguishes pre-field and field techniques. It describes one-to-one cognitive interviews using think-aloud, probing and paraphrasing, and explains how methods complement one another. The page was last revised on 8 October 2021. Its institutional requirements are not mandatory rules for other product teams, and no fixed participant count or problem-discovery percentage is adopted here.

Suppose a team asks staff about recent processing volume to decide whether workload deserves further investigation. A complete number establishes that the system received an answer. It does not establish that the number uses the intended period, role and status. If some respondents interpret “processed” as handled at any point while others mean finally completed, an orderly dataset cannot reconstruct the missing interpretations afterward.

Cognitive pretesting makes the response process inspectable. Understanding the question, recalling events, finding records, deciding which entries count and mapping a result to the available options can each introduce problems. Asking only “Was that easy to understand?” compresses those different stages into another general evaluation.

A complete synthetic ledger

The following question and records form an unexecuted design fixture. No participant has been recruited or tested. The question is: “During the past 30 days, from 8 October through 6 November inclusive, how many applications did you complete as the primary handler? Exclude withdrawn applications and applications returned for additional information.” Options are 0, 1–5, 6–10, 11 or more, and Cannot determine from available records. The dates and counts are hypothetical, not site statistics.

Provide five synthetic entries: one completed on 7 October; one completed on 8 October; one completed on 6 November; one withdrawn on 6 November; and one completed on 7 November. Assume the test role was the primary handler for all five. The independently specified answer is two completed applications, mapped to 1–5. The preceding day, following day and withdrawn entry are excluded. This is a designer’s boundary check, not an answer to reveal before the session.

First let the person respond in the intended survey mode and preserve the original choice. Then ask them to explain the start date in their own words, identify the records they used and excluded, and explain their choice of range. With real business material, establish whether records exist and whether access is authorized. Do not demand private records merely to observe retrieval. This small ledger checks the study input; it does not show that equivalent records are available in real work.

Keep the initial answer, included entries, interpretation of dates and statuses, moderator assistance and current research hypothesis separate. A response of 1–5 is insufficient evidence of correct interpretation. Counting two, incorrectly counting three, or guessing can all produce the same option. The captured option may be accurate as a record of the response while remaining inadequate evidence of shared meaning.

Give the methods different jobs

When the uncertainty concerns meaning, recall strategy or record sources, use a session that can probe the response process. When it concerns invitations, real-world burden, branching or device interaction, plan a suitable field pilot and usability work. They can be combined, but distinguish conclusions based on explanations, observed operation and distributions. “Pretested” is too broad to identify which gaps have been addressed.

The appendix explains that missingness and response distributions provide useful clues whose interpretation depends on context. A blank can indicate unavailable records, inapplicability or a real zero. A high completion rate can also contain forced guesses. Making a field required reduces empty entries without necessarily improving the underlying answer. Technical completeness does not establish a common measurement concept.

A field pilot is not an inferior method. It can expose record-retrieval costs, routing and operational conditions absent in a controlled session. Cognitive interviewing likewise need not always mean concurrent narration in a laboratory: the appendix includes concurrent and retrospective approaches. Select a combination rather than expecting one method to guarantee what it cannot observe. The existing article on double-barrelled questions addresses combining concepts within a question; this article addresses the evidence used to evaluate answering.

Decide what to do with a disagreement

Locate the stage before changing the wording. Uncertainty about the dates points toward the reference-period explanation. Correct interpretation with unavailable records points toward data access or an acceptable estimation method. Available records without a suitable option indicate a mapping problem. Describing all of these as “the user did not understand” invites a cosmetic wording fix to a different problem.

After a change, use equivalent new material rather than testing whether a participant remembers the earlier answer of two. Retain the questionnaire and material versions, session mode and assistance. A small pretest can reveal a concrete obstacle but cannot establish its population prevalence. Agreement among researchers is not additional respondent evidence.

A next step might be another cognitive session, a field pilot of the complete route, or suspension of a field that respondents cannot reliably answer. Connect the choice to an unresolved explanation. A correct answer to a synthetic ledger is not evidence that real workload has been measured accurately or that a resource investment is justified.

Can probing happen after the answer rather than during it?

Yes. Retrospective probing preserves the initial response before asking about interpretation, retrieval and selection. It reduces interruption during the first answer but can lose detail or introduce retrospective rationalization. Record the session condition and limitation rather than combining both modes as though they were one measurement or assuming a later account perfectly reconstructs the process.

A useful delivery includes the original question and target population, independent fixture expectations, respondent explanations, original answers, moderator interventions, unresolved hypotheses and the next methodological choice. Without actual sessions, it remains a research plan. Define what the pilot and the cognitive pretest each need to answer before combining them, so that “complete answers received” does not become “shared interpretation verified.”
