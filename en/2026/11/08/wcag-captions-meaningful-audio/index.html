Plan captions for speech, speakers and meaningful sounds

Use an eight-second synthetic script and three expected cues to review speech, speakers, an error tone and the text-alternative exception.

M Midnight Engineering notes on development, quality, security, delivery, and operations. View Author Profile
Updated 2026-11-08 · Estimated read 14 min
Translations
zh-CN en

Article

Key takeaways

  • Captions include necessary speech, speaker information and non-speech audio.
  • Script timings and file presence do not establish synchronization in delivered media.
  • The text-alternative exception needs equivalent information and clear labeling.

Captions for a training video need more than a copy of the dialogue. They must convey the speaker information and meaningful sounds needed to understand it. If the presenter says nothing when an error alarm sounds, a dialogue transcript can omit the outcome. Review the audio and picture timeline rather than merely checking that a caption file exists.

Identify the information carried by sound

WCAG 2.2 Success Criterion 1.2.2, Captions (Prerecorded) requires captions for prerecorded audio in synchronized media, with an exception for a clearly labeled media alternative to text that adds no information. It is Level A. This article uses the Recommendation dated 12 December 2024 and does not extend this single criterion into complete requirements for live or audio-only media.

The W3C Understanding document explains that captions include dialogue, necessary speaker identification and information conveyed by sound, including meaningful effects. The question is what affects understanding, rather than how many sounds occur. Incidental background noise and an alarm indicating failed submission can carry very different responsibilities.

Speaker labels depend on context too. A continuously visible single presenter need not necessarily have their name repeated for every line. With alternating voices over a screen recording, the speaker can affect the meaning of an instruction. Combining all speech into one paragraph can preserve words while losing their relationships. Captions should also avoid obscuring relevant visual information.

An eight-second synthetic review input

The following eight-second teaching script has not been filmed or rendered. It is not an alignment record for real media. From 0–2 seconds, an agent says “Send now.” From 2–3 seconds, an error tone sounds without speech. From 3–5 seconds, an administrator says “Check the address.” From 5–8 seconds, the operation is shown silently. Listing the auditory events makes it possible to specify caption coverage independently.

WEBVTT

00:00.000 --> 00:02.000
Agent: Send now

00:02.000 --> 00:03.000
[Error tone]

00:03.000 --> 00:05.000
Administrator: Check the address

The independent expectation is three cues conveying the first speaker, the error sound and the second speaker. Do not invent dialogue for the final silent interval. These timings come from a synthetic script. Finished media would need adjustment against its actual audio; this text cannot be described as already synchronized captions. Language, readability, style and actual playback conditions still need review.

Nor does the silent interval automatically receive a complete alternative. If the image shows an important step absent from the audio, these captions do not convert that visual information into auditory information. Evaluate the applicable media requirements separately. Coverage of all sounds does not establish that the entire video is accessible; visual information and player controls retain their own requirements.

A review input can retain script version, auditory event times, cue text, speaker, important visual information and evidence status. Initially these are plans. Add real timing and playback results only when media exists. Replacing narration or outsourcing a new edit can invalidate the original alignment even when the filename stays the same.

How a transcript creates false completeness

Suppose automatic transcription accurately records “Send now” and “Check the address” but skips the alarm. A viewer reading silently sees continued guidance without knowing that the first send failed. If both voices are assigned to one speaker, the correction between roles can disappear as well. These are planned negative cases, not results from a transcription service or an accuracy benchmark.

Adding a generic “sound effect” label everywhere is not a sufficient content decision. Identify the meaning of the sound and present the necessary information at its actual time. If it signals an error without explaining its cause, do not invent “Invalid address” as the caption. Preserve what the audio conveys; a causal explanation needs an actual message or other supporting content.

Open captions remain visible, reducing reliance on a toggle but making them harder to disable or replace. Closed captions offer choice through a supporting player, which must actually load the track and make its controls operable. Choose according to the delivery channel and users. Exporting a file alone does not establish that closed captions work, while text burned into an image is not a selectable language track.

Evidence belongs to the delivered player

After production, compare the auditory event list and captions throughout the complete video. Check missing speech, speaker changes, meaningful effects and synchronization. Then view it silently to assess whether the first send’s error and the instruction to check the address remain understandable. This verifies the fixture’s auditory information, not every training objective in a real course.

Also examine track selection, loading failures, narrow-screen obstruction and control access in the actual delivery player. After transcoding or replacing the video, check media identity and version against the caption track. Starting playback is not proof that captions appeared. This article has no real video asset and has run no playback test; these steps remain an acceptance plan.

See audio-control conditions for the separate issue of stopping automatic sound. Being able to pause audio and being able to obtain information without hearing it answer different questions. Separate records help prevent a full set of player buttons from concealing missing content.

Does a transcript below the video satisfy the caption requirement?

Normally, prerecorded synchronized media still needs synchronized captions. Evaluate the exception only when the video adds no information beyond the text and is clearly labeled as its alternative. A nearby document about a similar procedure does not automatically make the video a media alternative for that text. Compare the information, not just matching titles.

If the transcript omits this example’s error sound and role change, “instructions already provided nearby” cannot replace the comparison. Conversely, a clearly labeled video presenting only existing page text should be evaluated on that actual condition. Retain the reason and version supporting an exception, then reassess if an additional spoken explanation is introduced.

Before delivery, retain the auditory inventory, actual captions and target-player results so that a reviewer can trace where necessary information went. A script not yet produced remains a script; a file not yet played remains unverified. Only matching content, timing and delivery conditions support a caption result within this scope.
