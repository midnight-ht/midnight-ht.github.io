Choosing open, closed or hybrid sorting for a help centre

Use six help-content cards to compare the evidence from open, closed and hybrid sorts, then carry disputed groupings into finding tasks.

M Midnight Engineering notes on development, quality, security, delivery, and operations. View Author Profile
Updated 2026-11-08 · Estimated read 14 min
Translations
zh-CN en

Article

Key takeaways

  • Open, closed and hybrid sorts answer different structural questions.
  • Supplied category names cannot be reported as spontaneously generated.
  • Grouping informs a navigation hypothesis; finding content needs a separate task.

Choose an open, closed or hybrid card sort by the evidence the help-centre team needs: users’ own groupings, placements within an existing structure, or gaps in proposed categories. Supplying category names changes the question a study can answer. A completed sort also does not establish that people can find the content on a real site.

Who supplies the categories?

The Ontario government’s card-sorting guide distinguishes open sorting, where participants create and name groups; closed sorting, which supplies categories; and hybrid sorting, which allows new categories alongside supplied ones. It was published on 5 March 2018 and updated on 27 March 2025. These distinctions are useful here without treating the guide’s suggested card limit as a universal validity threshold.

Suppose a help centre follows an organization chart: Orders, Delivery and Billing. A new page explains how to supply missing refund evidence. Product places it under Orders; support staff expect After-sales. If participants receive only the three existing categories and must choose one, their placements describe choices within that supplied structure. They cannot establish that users spontaneously proposed those three categories or rejected an absent After-sales category.

Use an open sort when category ideas are still unknown, a closed sort when checking additions to a largely established structure, and a hybrid sort when existing categories may be incomplete. These methods are not successive levels of maturity. Tool-generated diagrams do not restore alternatives excluded from the study inputs.

A complete six-card input

This is an unexecuted research design with no recruitment, sessions, population percentages or success-rate results. The six cards are Request a refund, Track a refund, Supply refund evidence, Change an address, Delivery problem and Download an invoice. Assign a stable identifier to each and preserve its wording. Keep the cards at a comparable content level rather than mixing departments, complete pages and vague verbs.

The open version supplies the six cards and blank category labels. Participants form meaningful groups and name them. The closed version supplies the same cards plus Orders, Delivery and Billing, explicitly identified as the team’s categories. The hybrid version additionally permits new categories. Identical cards do not make results interchangeable: the category source and available choices differ.

Specify expectations before the exercise without prescribing user answers. The open version has no required category names; an unexpected label or an unplaced card should be recorded. For the closed version, an independent review should ensure that the report never describes supplied names as spontaneously generated. For the hybrid version, distinguish reused, renamed and new categories. These expectations evaluate the study’s claim boundaries rather than predicting where participants will place cards.

A record should link participant codes, card identifiers, group identifiers and labels, supplied-category status, unplaced items and explanations. Preserve important reasons for moves rather than keeping only a colourful exported board. Do not immediately explain that Orders “obviously includes refunds.” That explanation changes the interpretation being observed. The guide likewise advises against resolving unclear labels or cards on the participant’s behalf.

Do not summarize away the disagreement

Imagine two synthetic participants creating Refunds and After-sales. This is an analytical illustration, not collected research. An editor can hypothesize overlap, but merging the labels does not establish agreement on After-sales. One group might include invoice corrections; the other might contain refund items alone. Compare membership, explanations and task context before calling the names synonyms.

Matching names can also conceal different meanings. Orders may include the entire purchasing lifecycle for one person and only completed purchases for another. A stable-looking aggregate may reflect a shared label rather than a shared model. Keep the disagreement and remaining questions visible instead of voting an unexplained grouping into the only correct navigation, particularly when user roles have different tasks.

Card selection also constrains the result. Six short titles omit site search, context, authentication conditions and similar-looking real content. Including After-sales in the card wording may influence labels; deleting it may make the item unrecognizable. Document the selection and check basic comprehensibility rather than repeatedly editing away every difference to obtain tidy groups.

Test finding separately from grouping

A card sort can inform a candidate structure. A subsequent study should ask whether users can find an entry while pursuing a credible goal. For example: “Your refund application was returned because a photograph was missing. Find where to supply it and continue.” Supply necessary context without naming Orders or After-sales. Observe the first path, returns, failure and assistance. See writing neutral usability tasks for the separate problem of not revealing the route.

This finding task has not been run either. Its expected endpoint differs from the sort: locating the actual evidence entry, recognizing the relevant application and being able to continue. If the prototype lacks a working submission, restrict the endpoint to location and understanding. Do not report a completed refund process. Permissions and business conditions cannot be inferred from arrangements on a card table.

Use disagreements to choose subsequent tasks. If refund versus invoice boundaries are unclear, prepare distinct materials for both goals rather than testing only an easy refund route. A report should identify the card version, method, participation conditions and uncovered content so that a navigation proposal has traceable support.

Should a card be forced into one category?

If overlapping mental models matter, record multiple placements or uncertainty before deciding whether the site needs cross-links. Forced placement removes that signal. A tool allowing only one location can be supplemented with a question about where else the person would look. Do not present a tool limitation as a limit on user understanding.

The product may still need one primary content owner for maintenance and permissions. That does not require one user entry point. Decide on cross-links, search aliases or multiple routes using actual finding tasks and maintenance costs. Deliver a sort whose method and disagreements remain visible, then turn the most contested entry into a subsequent task. That makes the decision more inspectable than publishing the category with the highest vote count.
