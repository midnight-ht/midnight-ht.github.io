When architecture changes, keep the superseded ADR

Preserve the old decision, link its replacement, and separate architectural intent from migration completion.

M Midnight Engineering notes on development, quality, security, delivery, and operations. View Author Profile
Updated 2026-10-10 · Estimated read 6 min
Translations
zh-CN en

Article

When an architectural decision changes, usually retain its ADR and identify the replacement. Deletion removes context for existing systems; rewriting history makes unmigrated code appear mistaken from the beginning. An ADR records one decision and its rationale, not every implementation detail.

History and current instructions serve different purposes

Michael Nygard’s 2011 article proposes small, sequentially numbered records containing context, decision, status and consequences. Reversed decisions remain, marked superseded and linked to replacements. This is a useful method rather than the only architectural standard or a newly published specification.

Imagine synchronous report generation chosen when data was small and brief waits were acceptable. Later approval and recovery responsibilities motivate asynchronous jobs. Replacing “synchronous” in the old document hides why blocking interfaces still exist and why avoiding a queue was once reasonable. This hypothetical example contains no measured performance or migration result.

The new record should identify changed assumptions, feasible alternatives and added responsibilities. A queue introduces redelivery, expiration and ownership questions. “Business growth” alone gives future reviewers little basis for reconsideration.

Superseded does not mean migrated

Decision acceptance, implementation, traffic migration and historical-data handling are separate states. Link the migration plan rather than embedding every task in the ADR. Operators still need instructions reflecting the environment currently running.

Place a replacement notice in the old record, reference it from the new one, and specify affected systems. If domains require different choices, describe partial coverage instead of inventing a universal replacement chain. Identify retained scenarios and responsibility at their boundary.

Records also cost maintenance. Documenting every rename hides consequential choices. As an editorial recommendation, prioritize cross-team contracts, difficult-to-reverse data choices, deployment responsibility and significant quality tradeoffs. Teams can adapt that filter; even short records should explain consequences rather than popularity.

Define reconsideration triggers

If asynchronous processing adds storage and cleanup work, state which deadline or ownership changes warrant another review. Do not invent throughput thresholds; actual numeric triggers need sources and observation windows.

A reviewer should find today’s applicable decision and understand yesterday’s implementation. Link requirements and design decisions to evidence and migration tasks while preserving their separate purposes and update histories.
