A smooth demo does not prove usability: write tasks without giving the answer

State the business goal, necessary materials, correct outcome and observation plan without giving the interface route; distinguish independent completion from moderator help, recovery and uncovered conditions.

M Midnight Engineering notes on development, quality, security, delivery, and operations. View Author Profile
Updated 2026-11-26 · Estimated read 18 min
Translations
zh-CN en

Article

Key takeaways

  • A task should provide a believable business goal and enough context, not menu names, button labels or a sequence of interface actions.
  • Define the correct business outcome before testing; a success banner does not prove that the right record or next state was reached.
  • A moderator may help a participant continue, but must preserve the pause, attempts and failure before help and report assisted completion separately.
  • An observed problem is not its cause; participant experience, prototype capability, data realism and assistive technology affect what can be inferred.

A usability task that does not give away the answer should state the business problem and provide the context and materials needed to understand it. Do not name the interface route, button or action sequence. If the moderator supplies a menu location, later progress can only count as assisted completion. The expense-report prototype below is hypothetical; no participants were recruited, no prototype was run, and no completion rate was collected.

Business goals and interface answers are different kinds of information

Consider a leading task: “Open Expense Management, click Add Claim, choose Travel, and upload your receipt.” It has already supplied the information architecture, control labels and business classification. Even if someone completes it smoothly, the study has not observed whether they can find the entry point, understand the expense type, or locate the attachment control. A better task is not deliberately vague. It might say: “You have just returned from a work trip with the tickets for the journey and the company reimbursement policy. Complete the reimbursement submission using these materials and check that the resulting state matches what you expected.” The participant still has to interpret the evidence, explore the service and decide what to do.

GOV.UK’s moderated usability testing guidance describes the method as watching participants try to complete specific tasks using a service. It recommends tasks with a clear goal that is credible and relevant to participants, challenging enough to reveal issues, and free of clues about the answer or route. A discussion guide can standardize task descriptions, introductions and session records; researchers can try the instructions with a colleague and use them consistently. This article applies those task-design principles. It does not treat government recruitment or session arrangements as fixed quotas for every product team. (GOV.UK guide)

Neutral does not mean context-free. Participants need to know why the task matters, which materials they may use, and what outcome the business needs. For an expense claim, the task might provide a date, itinerary and receipts, and explain that company policy requires the relevant costs to be submitted. Leave the product’s category name—“Travel” or “Transport,” for example—and the approver field undisclosed. If the study is about understanding terminology, translating the term in the spoken instructions can remove the very difficulty the team meant to observe.

Define a correct outcome before calling a task complete

Before a session, agree on the business outcome with product and finance stakeholders. In this scenario, is clicking Submit enough? Or must the researcher verify the claimant, amount and receipt, as well as the pending-approval state? If a green banner is the only success condition, a participant may submit the wrong person’s expense or the wrong amount and still be counted as successful. The reverse is also possible: if the question is only whether participants can find the claim entry point, requiring an entire approval workflow adds unrelated ways to fail.

A task brief can separate the research question, participant role and preconditions, scenario and materials, allowed or prohibited assistance, business endpoint, behaviors to observe, and session stopping conditions. If the goal is to reach the correct pending state, the observer should verify that state in the prototype or record, rather than accepting “done” as proof. The participant-facing task and the moderator’s endpoint, hypotheses and data-collection plan should stay separate. This is an editorial research-design suggestion, not a mandatory template defined by GOV.UK.

A colleague read-through can uncover missing dates, receipts or role context and reveal whether a sentence accidentally names a navigation route. The colleague is not a study participant; their easy completion does not prove product usability. The manual also advises testing instructions before sessions and presenting tasks consistently. Actual evidence still requires participants and product conditions that fit the research question.

If a participant gets stuck, continue without erasing the original evidence

Refusing all help may leave a session stuck at one entry point, preventing observation of later steps. Moderators should use clear, neutral instructions and stay quiet as much as possible; if someone is completely stuck, the guidance allows a moderator to help them get back on track so learning can continue. The useful distinction is not whether the moderator performed a ritual of silence. It is whether the help, its timing and the participant’s state are recoverable from the record.

A session note can separate at least four things: the business outcome reached independently; pauses, backtracking and attempts before a prompt; the exact words or operational help the moderator provided; and whether the participant reached the goal afterward. When someone stalls, an open question such as “What are you thinking right now?” or “What did you expect to happen here?” preserves more evidence than “Did you find the upload button in the upper-right corner?” The second question names an interface element; the resulting click does not show independent discovery.

Suppose a participant mistakes the recipient account for the claimant. First record what appeared on screen, what the person said and the route they tried. Then list competing explanations: similar field labels, an unexpected information order, missing policy context, or a prototype that fails to make the current role clear. “The user was careless” is neither an observation nor a testable cause. A later task or interface change should distinguish between these possibilities.

Data realism, participant experience and assistive technology limit conclusions

The manual favors participants’ own data and documents when possible because this can increase engagement and reveal more contextual issues, provided the service can process that information securely. If real information cannot be used safely, researchers can create dummy materials. Dummy data remains useful, but participants may be less engaged and some contextual problems may not surface. A hypothetical expense study with synthetic trips and receipts could examine information structure; it would not prove that real financial records, blurry photos or company-specific approval rules were covered.

Recruitment should match the research question. A study of first-time expense submission may miss onboarding barriers if it includes only colleagues who know the internal system. Devices and assistive technology also matter. The official guide notes that personal configurations, such as screen readers or speech recognition, can be difficult to reproduce; researchers should understand the relevant technology so they do not mistake a service problem for a device or research-environment issue. Recruitment fit and task neutrality are separate controls. See how recruitment limits study conclusions; distinguish the credibility of the task itself from validating a structure after card sorting.

Retest the obstacle, not a memorized route

If the first session reveals that participants cannot find the submit entry point, then the same person using the same receipt after a navigation change may succeed simply because they remember the route. Keep the business goal but use equivalent new materials to see whether the barrier still appears. Record the task wording, prototype version, participant conditions, moderator assistance and observed result before and after a change. Without those boundaries, a team can mistake familiarity for repair.

Keep observations separate from interpretations. “The participant paused at the claim list and opened help” is an observation. “The label may not match their understanding of reimbursement” is a possible interpretation. The next study should say how it will distinguish terminology, ordering and policy understanding. One session can uncover a barrier worth investigating; it cannot by itself say how many users experience it or replace a population estimate with a defined sampling and quantitative method. Any reported rate needs its own sample, recruitment and denominator; a small set of observations should not be presented as a population completion rate.

Review the task itself before the session: does the participant know why the work matters? Are the background and materials sufficient? Does any sentence reveal a menu, button or route? Has the team specified the correct business endpoint? Will a moderator preserve independent failure if help is given? These checks say more about whether the study tested the product than whether a demonstration looked smooth.
