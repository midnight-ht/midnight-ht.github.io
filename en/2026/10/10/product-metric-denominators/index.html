More clicks, fewer completed tasks? Define the denominator first

Define the user benefit, business unit, denominator, outcome states, and observation window before treating a rise in clicks or funnel conversion as task success.

M Midnight Engineering notes on development, quality, security, delivery, and operations. View Author Profile
Updated 2026-11-26 · Estimated read 24 min
Translations
zh-CN en

Article

Key takeaways

  • Define the benefit, business object, and completion rule; keep numerator and denominator in the same unit.
  • Use a fixed observation window and separate completed, not completed, pending, and immature cohorts; a missing event is not automatically a failure.
  • Validate against business state, retries, late data, and definition versions before using a metric to guide decisions.

Product metrics should start with what users accomplish, before counting clicks. More clicks can mean an easier-to-find entry point; they can also mean that the same task is being submitted repeatedly. Without a defined business unit, completion state, and observation window, a precise-looking conversion rate may describe a different population than the team thinks.

Turn the benefit into a falsifiable hypothesis

Suppose a team moves an offline document request to a web form. More visits show that more people reached the entry point; they do not show that applicants received the correct document sooner. A closer statement of user value might be: among eligible requests, more people receive the correct document within the promised time, without shifting work to support or reviewers. Review queues, inventory, and offline processing also affect the outcome, so a before-and-after line alone cannot establish that the form caused a change.

The HEART paper from Google Research describes mapping product goals to user-centered metrics. The GOV.UK service metrics guide similarly moves from service purpose and user needs to benefits, hypotheses, and measurements, and recommends combining user research with multiple data sources. This article adopts that reasoning order—why first, how to count second. It does not treat HEART categories or UK government mandatory KPIs as universal rules for companies.

A falsifiable hypothesis could be: “Reducing repeated form entry will increase the share of eligible requests that receive the document within fourteen days, without increasing rejection or repeated support contacts.” It names the intended benefit, the time boundary, and possible side effects. Only then can the team ask whether the denominator is a person, one document need, each form submission, or a web session. Each can be counted correctly, but they answer different questions.

The denominator selects a statistical unit

“Users” answers how many distinct people did something. “Events” answers how many times an action occurred. “Sessions” describes behavior within a visit. A business object might be an application, order, ticket, or appointment. One person can make multiple requests, one request can generate several submissions, and one session can include multiple tasks. Put these units into the same percentage without an explicit mapping and two apparently identical metrics stop being comparable.

Amplitude’s Metric Explorer exposes Unique Users, Event Totals, and session measurements as distinct choices. In one interval, a person who triggers an event several times counts once under Unique Users and once per occurrence under Event Totals. Its funnels also let an analyst set event order and a conversion window. The Amplitude measurement guide and funnel interpretation guide describe that product’s behavior, not the only valid semantics for a business workflow. Analytics software can make a counting choice visible; it cannot decide what “done” means to the user.

Before choosing a denominator, write a metric contract: What is the counted object? Who is eligible to enter? When does it first enter? Which repeated actions are merged? What do success, rejection, withdrawal, and pending mean? When does the observation window start? How are late or missing events handled? Which definition version is in force? Above all, keep numerator and denominator in the same unit. If the denominator is requests, the numerator must be completed requests from that population, not distinct users who completed at least one task.

A cross-day example: wait for a complete window

The following numbers are hypothetical and are included only to explain the counting rule. A service has one hundred distinct, eligible document requests, each submitted for the first time. Retries and network recovery create one hundred and twenty form submissions across those requests. The team defines the outcome as “the request receives the correct document within fourteen days of its first submission.” In a cohort whose full fourteen-day window has elapsed, sixty-five requests reach that outcome, twenty are rejected or withdrawn within the window, and fifteen remain in progress at day fourteen. The on-time completion rate is therefore sixty-five divided by one hundred, or 65 percent. The outcome breakdown must still preserve completed, not completed, and pending as distinct states.

Using the one hundred and twenty submissions as the denominator, the same sixty-five completed requests would yield 54.2 percent. That is not an arithmetic error. It describes completions per form submission; retries increase its denominator and make it penalize requests that needed another attempt. That rate can help answer “How many completed requests does each form action produce?” It is a poor fit for “Did people with a document need receive it?” Decide before inspecting the redesign result.

Now consider the data cutoff. Suppose twenty other requests were submitted only five days ago. Their fourteen-day window has not elapsed, so the lack of a completion event does not establish eventual failure. Counting them early would depress the latest completion rate and create an apparent decline. Excluding them without disclosing the maturity rule could make recent results look better. A more defensible report groups requests by first-submission date, compares cohorts that have each completed the full fourteen-day observation window, and displays the immature count and latest observable date beside the rate. Fourteen days is not a default for every product; choose the window from the user-facing service deadline, outcome latency, and decision cadence.

Retries need a stable request identifier, not a browser session. The business service can issue or retrieve a unique object ID when it creates a request. Each page event can carry an appropriately controlled or pseudonymous analytics key; names and identity-document numbers do not belong in every event. Deduplicate repeated events with an event ID or idempotency key. Confirm receipt from the actual delivery record or a trusted business state source, rather than inferring it from a button click. The analytics deduplication rule must reproduce the business state, or the same event may count once in one chart and twice in another.

Missing, late, and rejected are different outcomes

A missing terminal event can mean several things: the business state has not updated, a message is delayed, instrumentation was lost, object IDs failed to join, or the request truly did not complete. Calling every absent completion event a failure turns pipeline trouble into an apparent product problem. Excluding all such rows hides genuine incompletion. The metric contract therefore needs an explicit unknown state and data-quality counts, such as objects without terminal states in each cohort, mismatches between the business database and analytics warehouse, and event-delay distribution. Also document when historical results freeze and when late events may revise them; otherwise yesterday’s metric can keep changing without explanation.

A small reconciliation can reveal many definition problems. Select a sample of business request IDs and compare first-submission time, eligibility, delivery state, and event sequence. Check whether retries for one object are merged, whether a rejection is misread as success, and whether late events are cut off by the window. This sample is not a significance test and cannot prove that every event is correct, but it can reveal a mismatch between the definition and its implementation. Before release, inspect two boundary cases: an object with no terminal event, and an object completed in the final hours of the window whose event reaches the warehouse the next day. If the dashboard cannot explain how it treats them, do not use it as a team target yet.

What to do after clicks rise depends on other evidence

Suppose clicks rise after a redesign, the fourteen-day completion rate is unchanged, and support receives more duplicate questions. Do not declare an engagement win or immediately conclude that the interface failed. First check duplicate client events by device and entry point. Then compare object counts and waiting time from form start through valid submission, review, and delivery. Finally, use user feedback or support reasons to test where the friction occurs. Each segment is a diagnostic clue, not proof of cause. Estimating the causal effect of an interface change requires a suitable experiment or comparison design, with concurrent policy, inventory, and marketing changes made explicit.

For repeat purchases, deduplicating by user can undercount orders. For a person trying to obtain one document, counting each click can overstate the number of opportunities. For page depth during one visit, a business object may not be the right unit. There is no shortcut such as “always use users” or “all funnels count events.” Choose in this order: define the user task and observable outcome; select a unit that corresponds to that task; then document how the analytics tool implements the count.

Add a balancing view. Alongside on-time completion, a team might monitor rejection, pending work, repeated submissions, and corrections or contacts after delivery, while naming each data source. The GOV.UK guide specifically recommends combining web analytics with feedback, performance, and offline-channel evidence. Applied to a company service, that means online clicks cannot stand in for the offline result. It does not mean every team needs the same number of KPIs. More charts do not necessarily produce a clearer decision; each line should inform a concrete judgment or action.

Validate before instrumentation becomes a target

Treat the definition as a set of business rules, not merely a chart caption. Confirm the object ID lifecycle across creation, retry, recovery, and withdrawal. Decide whether event time means client occurrence or server confirmation. Measure late-event frequency and its effect on the conclusion. Sample analytics rows against business state. Replay historical objects through the proposed calculation and confirm that the numerator, denominator, window, and exclusions explain each sample. Until those checks have actually been run, describe the plan as unverified; do not present hypothetical figures as measured results.

Version definition changes with an effective date. For example, moving from “submit button clicked” to “request accepted by the service” must not silently splice unlike values into one trend. If old data cannot be reliably recalculated, show a break and separate the old and new definitions. If it can be recalculated, retain the rule version and replay record. A review should ask not only whether the line moved, but whether the eligible population changed, the latest cohort matured, and data coverage remained stable.

When a team cannot say when a task begins or counts as complete, or its offline outcome is unavailable, the report should show a verifiable intermediate state and mark end-to-end completion as unknown. A more detailed chart cannot repair a missing business state. The denominator is ultimately a product definition that can be reviewed: it determines who enters the judgment, which outcomes count as success, and what evidence the team intends to act on. Read alongside service reliability indicators and incident learning, it helps separate “Did we solve the user’s problem?” from “Did the service reliably deliver the capability it promised?” without collapsing both into one score.
