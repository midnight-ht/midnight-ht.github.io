Multiwindow SLO alerts do more than repeat a threshold

The long SLO window gauges budget loss; the short window confirms errors still occur. Require both to limit stale pages after recovery.

M Midnight Engineering notes on development, quality, security, delivery, and operations. View Author Profile
Updated 2026-10-21 · Estimated read 17 min
Translations
zh-CN en

Article

Key takeaways

  • For a 99.9% SLO, 1.44% errors equal 14.4x burn; one hour is about 2% of a 30-day request budget only near average traffic.
  • Use AND within each long/short pair; OR can combine the fast and slow paging paths.
  • Sparse traffic can make one failure dominate the ratio; inspect counts and business importance.

A brief outage has ended, yet the on-call pager continues to fire: the past-hour error rate still contains requests from the incident. One proposal is to shorten the window to five minutes so the alert clears quickly. Another is to raise the threshold to reduce noise. Either change, when applied to a single window, can miss a fault that consumes a meaningful share of the error budget. A multiwindow alert gives the long window the question “is the impact worth interrupting someone?” and the short one “is it still happening?”

Convert error rate to budget burn first

Suppose a service has a 99.9% request-success SLO over thirty days. Its allowed error rate is 0.1%. If the observed error rate is 1.44%, the burn rate is 1.44% / 0.1% = 14.4: the budget is being spent 14.4 times as fast as an even allocation allows. Thirty days contain about 720 hours. Only if the eligible request volume in that hour is close to the cycle’s average hourly volume would sustaining that rate for one hour consume roughly 14.4 / 720 = 2% of the monthly request budget. A traffic spike or day-night variation changes the actual share; the ledger must count eligible and failed requests across the period. Nor does an observed window predict how long the rate will persist.

The Google SRE Workbook chapter on SLO alerting gives a paging example with a one-hour long window, five-minute short window, and 14.4x burn rate. It also presents a six-hour/thirty-minute pair at 6x. Six hours at 6x correspond to roughly 6 × 6 / 720 = 5% of a thirty-day budget only under the same near-average traffic assumption; actual consumption needs request counts. These examples show how impact and response speed can be paired; they are not universal configuration values. A different SLO period, traffic distribution, or critical user path calls for a new calculation and possibly a different notification route.

The denominator matters as much as the threshold. If a service handles health checks, sign-ins, and payments while the SLO covers completed payments, the alert expression must not use every HTTP request as its denominator. Health-check successes could conceal payment failures; gateway retries could inflate the request count in another direction. Define good events, total eligible events, and the observed scope before calculating burn. Missing or delayed telemetry must not silently become zero errors. Whether to pause changes after a budget is exhausted is a separate governance decision covered in the site's error-budget change policy. This article is about when to involve an on-call responder.

Let the long window judge impact and the short one judge recency

A fast-burn page can require both conditions: burn above 14.4x over the past hour and burn above 14.4x over the past five minutes. The long window prevents a few seconds of noise from immediately paging. The short window lets the alert clear after a recovered failure leaves the recent view, without waiting for all its errors to leave the one-hour average. This is an AND, not an OR. OR would let a short spike page on its own or let historical errors in the long window keep paging. AND is not a universal noise filter either; sparse traffic still needs sample-size and evaluation choices.

A slower but sustained loss can use a six-hour long window and thirty-minute short window at 6x. It may recognize damage later than the fast path, but it catches sustained losses that never cross the fast threshold. The combined page can be expressed as (1h AND 5m at 14.4x) OR (6h AND 30m at 6x): each pair uses AND, while either paging path may activate the overall alert. Severity cannot be named from arithmetic alone. A low-volume but high-value transaction might need a different page; a nightly batch may warrant a workday ticket. Choose routing with user impact, available response action, and on-call load in mind rather than copying a table into an unrelated product.

Consider this hypothetical replay, not an executed Prometheus query or alert run. A payment SLO sees ten thousand eligible requests over one hour, two hundred of them failed: a 2% error rate or 20x burn. The last five minutes contain one thousand requests and twenty failures, also 2% and 20x. Both windows exceed 14.4x, so the intended fast-burn alert state is firing. Also assume the oldest five minutes of the current hour contained one thousand requests and no failures. After an operator fixes the configuration, the next five minutes likewise have one thousand requests and no failures, replacing that oldest segment. The hour therefore still has ten thousand requests and two hundred failures, or 20x burn, while short-window burn falls to zero. The fast path’s internal AND is false and should resolve after evaluation and window progression; if the slower path remains true, the combined page has not resolved. The operator should record user impact and verify recovery rather than keep paging for the stale hour average. These are mathematical intended states. Real transitions also depend on sampling, query semantics, evaluation interval, and any for duration in the rule.

Reverse the situation: a fault has just produced two minutes of high recent errors, but many earlier successes keep the hour-long rate below the threshold. The AND does not page yet because this configuration uses budget-loss scale to decide whether to interrupt someone. If two minutes of this fault are already unacceptable, adjust the SLO, fast path, or a separate symptom alert. Quietly changing AND to OR would alter the policy rather than fix a query bug. Another invalid rule uses payment events in the long window but all HTTP requests in the short window. The two numbers then describe different SLIs; combining them can miss or invent an incident despite a precise-looking threshold.

Sparse traffic makes ratios jump

The Workbook specifically discusses low traffic. At ten requests per hour, one failure means a 10% hourly error rate. Against a 99.9% SLO, that is 100x burn. At ten requests every hour for thirty days, there would be roughly 7,200 requests and a monthly allowance of 7.2 failures; one failure uses about 13.9% of that allowance. The percentage is striking, yet the five-minute window may show an even higher instantaneous rate if its only request failed. If there are no requests in a five-minute window, its denominator is empty and it cannot supply a stable signal. Raising 14.4 to 20 does not systematically solve this sampling problem.

A low-volume service may use longer aggregation, a minimum event count, synthetic probes, or an event definition closer to user impact. Each option has a cost. A minimum count can delay detection of one critical customer's failure; a synthetic probe cannot stand in for actual user outcomes. If a single failed transaction is itself a major incident, define a separate event alert instead of forcing one statistical ratio to infer a sparse rate and monitor a critical transaction. Evaluate absent counters, resets, and zero-traffic behavior along with the windows. “No requests” must not be interpreted as “zero errors and healthy.”

Replay the notification rhythm before enabling paging

Before production, replay fixed historical or synthetic event sequences: a two-minute spike followed by recovery, an hour of severe errors, six hours of slower loss, one failed request at low volume, and a telemetry outage. Preserve raw eligible and failed request counts for each sequence; calculate both windows' burn, firing and resolution times, and whether the result should be a page, ticket, or silence. Check how evaluation interval, for, silence rules, and aggregation across regions or service labels alter those results. This article has not run the queries. The target monitoring system must provide the actual replay output before anyone claims the rule works.

Each alert also needs an action: what can the responder do when woken, how will they know users are still being harmed, and when should repeated notifications stop? Fast and slow multiwindow rules connect budget loss with a current fault. They cannot replace an accurate SLI, reliable telemetry, or a post-incident account of user impact. Once those boundaries and the replay results are recorded, the chosen thresholds can be judged as part of a real on-call policy.
