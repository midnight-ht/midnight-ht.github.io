Give feature flags an owner and a retirement condition

Define flag ownership and retirement while treating long-lived operational controls separately.

M Midnight Engineering notes on development, quality, security, delivery, and operations. View Author Profile
Updated 2026-10-14 · Estimated read 5 min
Translations
zh-CN en

Article

Give a feature flag a purpose, owner, and retirement condition. A temporary release flag can keep imposing maintenance and validation work after full rollout. A long-lived operational control, however, should not be deleted merely because it is old.

Rollout completion does not end branch costs

Pete Hodgson's original Feature Toggles essay discusses categories, conditional-logic and testing costs, and ways to constrain and remove flags. The complete article dates to 2016 with a 2017 revision; it is methodological evidence, not today's product announcement. Its incident examples are not experiences claimed by this site.

Imagine a new checkout fully enabled while a flag retains the old path. A later order-field change requires deciding whether both branches remain supported. Product may consider the old flow retired while engineering continues maintaining it. Record why it remains: temporary rollback and exceptional-user support need different ending conditions. “It might be useful” does not define responsibility.

Make retirement observable

At creation, propose a target population, expected lifetime, rollback window, and removal owner. Retirement covers code, tests, documentation, and other readers, not just a configuration key. Multiple services require an explicit removal order and behavior for missing configuration.

No cleanup or time-saving measurement was performed here. Automated reminders can identify overdue flags but do not authorize deletion. A date triggers reassessment; it does not prove users no longer require a behavior. Tie decisions and review to an actual version.

Long-lived controls need a different contract

Operational switches, authorization choices, and lasting business variants may need continued support. Review defaults, configuration provenance, change permissions, and failure behavior rather than imposing a short release expiry.

Validate supported settings and significant combinations without claiming universal coverage. Remove unsupported paths explicitly; an untested old branch is not reliable rollback evidence. See production responsibilities beyond a usable prototype. A flag enables controlled change, but an unclear lifetime preserves forgotten promises.
