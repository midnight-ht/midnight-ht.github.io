Field or lab user research? Start with the question you need answered

Field and lab studies answer different questions. Choose a setting for context discovery, interface comparison, or service-in-use evidence, then separate observation from interpretation.

M Midnight Engineering notes on development, quality, security, delivery, and operations. View Author Profile
Updated 2026-11-25 · Estimated read 29 min
Translations
zh-CN en

Article

Key takeaways

  • A field setting is not automatically more realistic: contextual observation finds workflow conditions but does not by itself prove product effectiveness.
  • Controlled sessions can compare interface behavior, but findings remain bounded by the task, materials, device, prompts, and participants.
  • Keep observed behavior, participant explanation, researcher hypothesis, and design action distinct, then validate consequential changes.

Field and lab research are not a choice between “real” and “unreal.” Write down the question the team needs answered, then choose the setting that can supply the relevant evidence: observe context when you need to discover how a task fits into everyday work; control a session when comparing interface versions; and evaluate a service in its relevant operating setting when the question concerns the service as a whole. One study may not answer all three questions.

Start with the decision the evidence must support

“Can users complete this task?” sounds precise, but it can mean several things. A product team may want to know whether people notice a control, understand a field, have to find material before starting, need a colleague's confirmation, or cannot continue because of a network or assistive-technology constraint. These questions have different units of observation. Clicks on a page do not explain the work before and after the page. A view of the whole workflow, on the other hand, may make it harder to tell whether a problem came from the wording, the layout, or an external condition.

Before designing a study, turn the question into a claim that could be checked: “For this role, task, and set of materials, can a participant complete step X without help?” That phrasing suits a controlled task comparison. For a question such as “Why do people pause before submission, and what do they do next to obtain missing information?”, observe the task among its materials, tools, and collaborators. To decide whether a service fits into an actual operating rhythm, examine its functions, timing, environmental constraints, and acceptability in that context as well. The more concrete the claim, the easier it is to see whether a proposed session can support it.

This is not a ranking of methods. State the limits of the evidence: who performed which task, using what device and materials, with what assistance, and in what environment. Turning “completed in one session in a meeting room” into “users can complete it” generalizes beyond the observed conditions. Turning “one wait was observed in the field” into “waiting is a universal pain point” makes the same mistake.

A controlled session can isolate interface questions, but it also removes conditions

The value of a controlled usability session is that important conditions can be made more comparable. Researchers can give participants the same task, similar materials, and consistent prompts, then observe how they understand controls, notice errors, and recover. When comparing field order or confirmation wording, this stability can help determine whether a difference is associated with the interface change. It can also make it easier to record the screen, note task stages, and review points of hesitation.

Stable conditions, however, are not a smaller copy of everyday use. Supplying an organized document may remove the question of where it came from. A pre-authenticated device, reliable network, and muted notifications may eliminate account switching or recovery after disconnection. A facilitator saying “go ahead” may move someone past a step where they would otherwise ask for help. The finding should therefore be written conditionally: “Participants completed the form when given the same material on a desktop browser,” rather than “The form has been validated.”

Control should not mean removing a target user's assistive technology or preferred interaction method simply to eliminate variation. W3C WAI's user-evaluation guidance notes that evaluation with real people can reveal usability issues that conformance checks alone do not uncover. User evaluation also cannot establish WCAG conformance; it should be combined with standards-based evaluation. Reports should state participant characteristics and study scope, and should not infer how all disabled people will use a service from a small number of sessions.

Contextual observation can reveal workflow, but it is not an effectiveness experiment

Contextual research places an activity back in an everyday setting such as home, school, or work, observing how people use their own equipment, real materials, and ordinary tools. In its contextual-research guide, GOV.UK describes it as a way to understand a wider problem, barriers in real-life use, and how people work around them. Observation can reduce omissions caused by relying entirely on memory: a participant can point to a process, document, or collaborator at hand. But the researcher still needs to decide what to observe. Visiting a place does not by itself make the evidence sufficient.

The amount of researcher intervention is itself a design choice. Quiet observation interferes less with the flow, but the researcher may not know whether an action is a habit, a temporary workaround, or a response to the setting. A timely question can clarify a reason but interrupt the activity. Asking someone to explain each step can give richer detail while changing what they normally do. The guide suggests choosing among these approaches according to the research objective and asking follow-up questions when appropriate. An interview answer is not a substitute for observing the step itself.

Contextual work especially benefits from separating observation from interpretation. “The participant left the page, opened a messaging app, then returned and re-entered the amount” is behavior that can be revisited. “The participant does not trust the service” is an interpretation. “Add an automatic receipt-reading feature” is a design action. The latter two may be right, but they need support from the participant's explanation, other sessions, or product evidence. The Office for Health Improvement and Disparities' contextual-inquiry guide recommends summarizing the researcher's understanding for the participant to confirm, and notes that analysis depends on researcher judgment. Having a second researcher independently review the material and compare disagreements is more reliable than turning the first interpretation directly into a requirement.

A published comparison shows why the objective matters, not which method always wins

In 2013, Sun and May published a study comparing laboratory and field evaluation of personalized mobile devices at large sports events. The abstract in the University of Nottingham repository says a lab experiment suited questions about interface and application-oriented usability, while the field experiment was better suited to broader factors affecting acceptance of the personalized mobile service in the event setting. That conclusion belongs to one study of a mobile service at sporting events and its research design; it is not a rule that field research is always better.

The example is useful because it separates levels of question. Whether an interface is easy to operate is not the same outcome as whether a service fits into the flow of watching an event. A separate example summarized by the Office for Health Improvement and Disparities in its contextual-inquiry guidance shows how observation can expose a gap between expectation and action: people could perceive digital material retrieval as quicker than finding physical resources, while the digital search sometimes took longer than intended. The official guidance says this inquiry produced three design implications for an online interruption-management product; it is not a rule for every task.

A lab can make device interaction easier to compare. A field setting adds timing, attention, and the event itself. If the decision is “When should information appear, and what activity must it not interrupt?”, task completion on an isolated screen is insufficient. If the decision is whether a button is easy to locate, field distractions may make attribution difficult. Method choice should return to the product decision at hand.

A hypothetical workflow shows how observation can lead to a test

The following is a planning scenario, not user research conducted for this article. Suppose a team wants to improve expense submission. In a meeting-room test, participants receive a prepared receipt image and can open a page, enter an amount, and submit it. That result only shows that the interface steps can be completed with supplied materials and the device used. It does not show how receipts usually enter the workflow or cover missing amounts, scattered materials, or a submission that requires another person's confirmation.

If the unknown is “Why do people pause before filling out the form?”, a first phase could observe one natural preparation process with the participant's consent. The researcher would write down what to investigate in advance: where the material comes from, when devices are switched, which steps require someone else's input, and how the participant resumes after an interruption. Imagine the notes said that the participant found an image in a phone message, moved to a desktop form, noticed a missing cost-center value, messaged a colleague, and left the page while waiting. The notes should preserve these actions and their sequence, rather than immediately rewriting the event as “Users need intelligent receipt recognition.” This is an illustrative note, not an actual observation.

Afterward, the researcher might ask whether the wait was unusual or part of the normal process, which materials are sensitive, and who typically supplies the cost center. If the participant describes an exceptional situation, the team should not elevate it immediately into a primary requirement. If a similar dependency appears in several sessions, the team must still report the participant and role scope rather than claiming a population percentage. One possible hypothesis might become “A user can resume after leaving the form, and the service identifies the next required piece of information,” rather than “Every receipt will be extracted automatically.” The first can be evaluated with a prototype. The second introduces accuracy, data-access, and error-handling questions that need separate technical and risk checks.

Only then might a controlled task compare two recovery designs: give participants the same hypothetical materials, introduce the same pause, and observe whether they find the unfinished step, understand the missing field, and avoid submitting twice. Do not treat the earlier observed wait as a success metric. Define observations and completion criteria beforehand, and check whether facilitator prompts affect the result. For broader service acceptability in an organization, return to the workplace or operational process to observe role handoffs and existing system constraints. Discovering a mechanism, comparing a design, and evaluating service operation can be a useful sequence, but no project is required to run all three phases.

Field and lab settings have different failure paths

Field research can fail because of the observer, the device, or recording arrangements. A researcher entering a setting may change what the participant does. Asking someone to open a personal device may expose private notifications, client material, or unrelated accounts. Connectivity, venue permission, and work schedules may also differ from the plan. Consent to participate does not automatically authorize recording, photography, copying materials, or remote streaming. For privacy, the GOV.UK notes and recording guide calls for informed consent before note-taking or recording, purpose-limited use, and secure storage; sensitive information should be de-identified, access limited, and unrelated material left unseen.

Controlled tests have their own sources of bias. Over-explaining, overly complete materials, or using only a research device may hide the very difficulty under investigation. A researcher may treat one person's workaround as a general pattern, or blame a new design for a failure without checking the device, assistive technology, browser, and setting. W3C WAI also cautions that evaluation with a few users cannot establish statistical significance or represent a broad population. Sometimes the sound conclusion is not “passed”; it is a precise statement of which variable the next study must distinguish.

Turn method choice into a validation chain

Planning can proceed in four steps. First, write down the decision that will depend on the research and the evidence claim that must hold. Second, define the task boundary: starting condition, materials, participant role, device, assistive technology, external collaboration, and environmental variation. Third, choose the observation setting and degree of intervention. To discover an unknown workflow, observe where the real steps are visible. To compare a single interface change, hold other conditions steady. To assess a service's fit, study how it interacts with the operating context. Fourth, define how observations will become a testable design hypothesis and what evidence would overturn it.

Keep at least four layers in the report: observable behavior, the participant's explanation, the researcher's current hypothesis, and the proposed product action. Each layer should link back to a participant identifier, task stage, and original note. De-identify personal information and restrict access according to the agreed protocol. If researchers disagree about an interpretation, retain the disagreement and alternative explanation instead of smoothing the evidence into a single theme. For a related treatment of evidence-led notes, see observations and interpretations in research notes.

Before turning a finding into a requirement, ask whether it changes a design choice. “The user switched from phone to desktop” is an event, not yet a requirement. It might imply cross-device continuation, a preference for typing on a computer, or that the material exists only on the phone. A follow-up session or prototype test must distinguish these explanations before the team knows what to build and how to evaluate it. A well-bounded conclusion may sound less certain, but it tells the reader what is known, what remains unknown, and which next piece of evidence is worth collecting.
