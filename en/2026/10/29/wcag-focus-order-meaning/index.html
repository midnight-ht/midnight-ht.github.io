Must WCAG focus order match every visual position?

Use recipient cards to evaluate meaningful focus order, task grouping, dynamic changes, correction, and more than one acceptable navigation sequence.

M Midnight Engineering notes on development, quality, security, delivery, and operations. View Author Profile
Updated 2026-10-29 · Estimated read 11 min
Translations
zh-CN en

Article

Key takeaways

  • Preserve meaning and operability through the sequence.
  • Organize navigation around task relationships.
  • Check dynamic changes as well as the initial route.

Two address cards sit side by side. Tab follows horizontal screen positions, alternating between different recipients’ fields. The route looks orderly on a diagram but makes ownership harder to follow. WCAG’s Focus Order criterion preserves meaning and operability; it does not turn pixel coordinates into one mandatory keyboard route.

What makes an order meaningful?

The WCAG 2.2 explanation of Focus Order describes SC 2.4.3, a Level A criterion. Where sequential navigation affects meaning or operation, focusable components receive focus in an order that preserves meaning and operability. Its table example allows either row-by-row or column-by-column navigation when the order reflects logical relationships.

Consequently, matching arrows in a design file is insufficient. The relationship between a recipient’s name, phone number, and Save button matters more than each control’s distance from the left edge. Difficulty identifying whose phone number will be saved is a reason to examine the sequence. A departure from left-to-right visual order is not automatically a failure.

Sequential focus navigation is also different from reading every piece of content. Ordinary paragraphs do not need to become Tab stops. Inspect screen-reader reading order, visual presentation, and keyboard focus separately against the same task. A focusable-element inventory does not establish that explanatory content is positioned meaningfully, and reading the full page does not establish that every action is reachable.

Follow one recipient through a complete edit

Consider an unimplemented, untested address book. Each of two cards contains a name, phone number, address, Save, and Delete. A problematic sequence visits both names, then both phone numbers, then addresses and actions. Moving from the first recipient’s name to the second’s name and back to the first’s phone can detach a field from its owner unless that relationship remains clear.

A candidate design groups the task: the first recipient’s fields and actions, followed by the second’s. The user edits and saves one recipient before entering the next card. A two-column visual layout can remain, while document structure and labeling follow each card. This is my recommendation for this hypothetical task, not a rule that all card interfaces must use this sequence.

A different product might primarily support checking every phone number in a batch. A table with clear row and column associations could better serve that task. Choose from actual work rather than treating permission for column navigation as approval of arbitrary interleaving. Multiple logical orders still require an explanation of the chosen relationship.

Avoid making a large collection of positive tabindex values the first repair. A separately maintained focus sequence can diverge from document structure, and adding a third card creates another ordering obligation. That is a maintenance argument, not a claim that the criterion universally bans positive values. Repairing a task-oriented structure can be easier to understand than maintaining a second ordering table.

Recheck the route after changes

A dynamic address book needs more than its initial screenshot. After Add recipient, define whether focus enters the new card, how its identity is communicated, and how reverse navigation reaches the initiating action. After Delete, choose a meaningful remaining location, such as a neighboring card or the empty list’s Add action. Do not rely on whatever position remains after removing the focused node. Confirmation, undo, and authorization remain separate responsibilities.

Saving an invalid phone number adds another route. If an error summary sits at the page top, specify how it returns users to the correct card and how editing continues to Save. A successful first-to-second-card route does not establish that errors retain recipient context.

Proposed verification starts from the page beginning, completes one card with forward Tab, and returns with reverse Tab. Repeat after adding a card, deleting one, and triggering a field error. Record the current recipient, focus target, and available operation. Repeat the task in the narrow single-column layout. No browser or assistive-technology testing was performed here; these are planned checks.

How should tests handle two reasonable orders?

Write constraints before prescribing one exact sequence: field ownership remains understandable, Save is reachable, correction stays associated with the current recipient, and dynamic changes do not send users to unrelated content. Then document representative forward and reverse paths for the implementation.

If row and column navigation both have plausible rationales, record their intended tasks and costs. Editing one recipient and checking all phone numbers are different activities; research is needed to establish which the product must prioritize. A hypothetical example cannot prove either choice works for its users.

Keep focus order separate from visibility. A meaningful first-card-to-second-card route can still place the active control behind a sticky toolbar. Inspect that independently; an acceptable route is not evidence of an unobscured focus state. See focus obscured by fixed regions.

Give testers the relationship constraints, representative routes, and dynamic states. Arrows alone can faithfully reproduce a route that splits one recipient’s task across several others.
