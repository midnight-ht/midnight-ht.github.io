Who selects the next Pod behind a Headless Service?

Separate DNS discovery from client selection and persistent connections, then compare a shared Service entry point with explicit member management.

M Midnight Engineering notes on development, quality, security, delivery, and operations. View Author Profile
Updated 2026-10-29 · Estimated read 11 min
Translations
zh-CN en

Article

Key takeaways

  • Headless discovery exposes endpoints without platform proxying or balancing for that Service.
  • Several DNS addresses do not migrate an established connection.
  • Observe discovery and fresh connections in the same client implementation.

A Headless Service returning several Pod addresses does not mean Kubernetes distributes every request evenly among them. It provides endpoint discovery; the client connects directly to its chosen address. Investigate DNS results, address selection and connection lifetime before expecting three replicas to receive one third of the traffic each.

Discovery changes the division of responsibility

The Kubernetes headless Service documentation states that explicit clusterIP: None allocates no cluster IP. Kube-proxy does not handle these Services, and the platform supplies no proxying or load balancing for them. With selectors, EndpointSlices and DNS expose backend Pod addresses. This uses documentation checked on October 29, 2026; no cluster or traffic distribution was tested.

Direct discovery is useful when a client needs member identity. It also makes endpoint selection, removal and failure handling explicit client responsibilities. A successful DNS lookup does not establish any of those behaviors.

An ordinary ClusterIP and a headless Service use different paths. Choose according to protocol requirements and client capability. An application that only understands a stable entry address does not acquire sophisticated endpoint management by switching to headless mode.

Three addresses and one persistent connection

Assume three eligible Pods in a test namespace, each labeled app=catalog and listening on port 8080. The unexecuted Service definition below requires separately prepared workloads and a client.

apiVersion: v1
kind: Service
metadata:
  name: catalog-peers
  namespace: test
spec:
  clusterIP: None
  selector:
    app: catalog
  ports:
    - name: http
      port: 8080
      targetPort: 8080

Suppose the client receives three endpoints, chooses the first entry and reuses one persistent connection. Concentrated traffic follows from that client policy. Other discovered addresses do not demonstrate that another connection was selected. This example neither predicts DNS ordering nor claims that all runtimes use this policy.

Now replace the selected Pod and allow DNS to update. An established connection does not migrate merely because a DNS record changes. Reuse may continue until closure or error. Re-resolution, caching and alternative selection depend on the implementation. A separate command-line lookup cannot establish that the business process consumed the new answer.

Capture both discovery results and actual connection destinations. New DNS addresses with old client destinations point toward connection pooling or discovery updates. Incorrect DNS endpoints instead call for selector, EndpointSlice and readiness inspection. Changing Service ports repeatedly does not repair connection reuse.

Client selection or a shared entry point?

If the protocol does not require member identity, an ordinary ClusterIP may suit a client that expects platform forwarding. It still does not promise strictly equal business requests across replicas; connection and protocol behavior remain relevant.

If direct membership is required, define the selection policy. Round-robin, load-aware and shard-aware choices solve different problems. Random selection can be wrong for a system whose members have distinct roles or data ownership.

Failure switching also depends on operation semantics. A connection failure before sending differs from a lost response after a write may have executed. Retrying at another address does not prove exactly-once execution. Headless discovery supplies no business deduplication protocol; use applicable operation identities and status reconciliation.

When should persistent connections select again?

That is a client lifecycle contract. Possible triggers include connection failure, an agreed lifetime, discovery changes or membership events. Short lifetimes increase reconnect work; long lifetimes can leave new replicas unused. Choose values from the protocol, client and measurements rather than from this hypothetical example.

Define behavior when discovery temporarily returns no endpoints. A bounded wait, explicit failure or protocol-specific use of known connections are different policies. Keeping a removed member indefinitely cannot be described as successfully adopting discovery updates. A live connection does not necessarily mean that member remains authorized to serve the operation.

Proposed checks observe the actual client through stable endpoints, additions, removal of its selected endpoint, connection failure and temporary absence of all endpoints. Record choices, connection creation times, retries and business outcomes. None were executed, and no balancing or failover success is claimed.

This differs from ExternalName aliases and TLS identity: the question here is client selection after direct endpoint discovery. Discovery, connection and the correct business result can fail independently. Evidence from each tells you whether to repair DNS, the client or the chosen Service abstraction.

Separate resolver caching from connection reuse with two controls in the same client implementation: log a fresh discovery result, then explicitly create a new connection and record its destination. A temporary tool in another runtime can use different caching or ordering. New connections using new endpoints while old connections remain unchanged point toward connection lifetime; stale fresh lookups point toward discovery refresh.
