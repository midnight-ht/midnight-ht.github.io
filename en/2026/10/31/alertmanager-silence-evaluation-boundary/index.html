Why is a Prometheus alert still firing after an Alertmanager silence?

Separate rule state from notification suppression, bound silence matchers with a finite example, and verify expiry and receiver behavior.

M Midnight Engineering notes on development, quality, security, delivery, and operations. View Author Profile
Updated 2026-10-31 · Estimated read 13 min
Translations
zh-CN en

Article

Key takeaways

  • Silencing notifications does not make the condition healthy.
  • Test both suppression and nonmatching controls.
  • Expiry is not guaranteed replay or service recovery.

An Alertmanager silence suppresses notifications for matching alerts. It does not make the Prometheus condition healthy. During maintenance, a quiet receiver and a firing alert can be an expected combination. Inspect rule state, silence matching, and receiver delivery separately before deciding which part failed.

The two systems maintain different state

The Alertmanager documentation on silences describes matcher-based suppression for a specified period. Alerts matching an active silence do not generate notifications. This operates on notification processing rather than pausing Prometheus evaluation. The online description was checked on October 31, 2026; no alerting system was connected and no silence was created.

The path includes rule evaluation, alert submission, Alertmanager matching and routing, and delivery to a receiver. Silencing belongs to the later portion. The earlier portion can continue producing a valid firing alert that remains visible in a dashboard. Stopping collection or withdrawing a rule requires its own mechanism and consequences.

This also limits recovery conclusions. Notification absence can result from a silence, routing changes, delivery failure, or an inactive alert. A quiet phone does not distinguish them. Conversely, firing during a silence is not evidence that notification suppression failed.

Define a finite maintenance scope

Consider an unexecuted example with three ApiError alerts. Their label pairs are service=checkout with environment=staging; service=checkout with environment=production; and service=search with environment=staging. Maintenance concerns only the first pair. The proposed silence requires both service=checkout and environment=staging, with an explicit start and end.

The first alert should be suppressed while the silence is active. The production alert should remain outside the scope despite sharing a service name. The search alert should remain outside despite sharing the staging environment. These controls can reveal the effect of accidentally omitting a matcher. They are defined expectations, not observed production behavior.

If alertname also defines the intended scope, include it explicitly and inspect the actual labels. Identical display names do not establish identical labels. Preserve the saved matchers, activity period, and maintenance explanation so another operator can understand the suppression.

A regular expression can widen the affected set beyond the intended service. Choose equality or pattern matching according to the maintenance object. For a multi-object expression, identify the expected matches and nearby nonmatches before testing it. Today's limited service inventory is not a substitute for a bounded matcher.

What is missing after silence creation succeeds?

Observe whether the test alert exists, whether it matches an active silence, and whether the intended notification is suppressed. If the alert never reached Alertmanager, receiving nothing proves nothing about the silence. Establish a working baseline on the same notification path before comparing active-silence and unsilenced conditions.

Retain a nonmatching control as well. Testing only that the target is quiet cannot reveal excessive suppression. An adjacent service and another environment should still follow their intended notification route. Perform this in an authorized test environment rather than creating customer impact. A useful record identifies the receiver, route, and observation window, not just the silence object.

Silencing and inhibition are also separate choices. A silence has an explicit period and matching scope. Inhibition suppresses related notifications when other alerts are present. Choose according to the maintenance window or the actual alert dependency; configuring one does not verify the other.

Who owns a maintenance extension?

If work cannot finish within the silence period, reassess the affected objects and remaining time before an authorized operator changes it. Widening scope and extending time are different decisions: one can suppress more services, while the other can delay detection of a new problem. Record the reason and owner for each.

A handoff should identify the original scope, current activity, next checkpoint, and alternative observations. The next responder needs to know which notifications will be absent and where to inspect the continuing condition. A chat message saying that the alert was silenced does not supply an end condition.

At completion, record silence removal and service recovery separately. Recovery with a leftover silence requires cleanup; expiry with a failing task requires continued response. Distinguishing these outcomes can expose notifications that remain suppressed after maintenance closes.

Does expiry immediately replay every historical notification?

Do not promise that behavior. Inspect currently active alerts and the notification pipeline after expiry rather than assuming that every suppressed historical occurrence will be replayed. Grouping and repetition have their own timing rules, described in the Alertmanager configuration reference. A silence end time is not a guaranteed receiver delivery time.

A proposed expiry test keeps a controlled condition active and records silence expiry, alert state, notification processing, and actual receipt. Compare it with a recovered condition so historical abnormality is not mistaken for a current problem. These tests have not been run, and no delivery latency is reported.

The keep_firing_for state window can separate a condition change from alert resolution. A silence acts later, on notification delivery. Recording those states side by side is more useful than writing only that an alert did not recover.

At maintenance completion, a responsible operator should review the remaining need for suppression, its agreed period, and the user-facing recovery checks. Extending a silence to make a dashboard quieter does not repair a service; expiry does not certify recovery. Record notification control and service recovery as separate decisions.
