A User Says ‘I’d Use It’: Reconstruct the Last Task Before Committing

An ‘I’d use it’ answer is a follow-up signal, not proof of demand or product effectiveness. Trace a recent task, separate recall from observation and inference, then choose the next research step.

M Midnight Engineering notes on development, quality, security, delivery, and operations. View Author Profile
Updated 2026-11-27 · Estimated read 21 min
Translations

Article

Key takeaways

  • Define the decision at stake, then reconstruct a specific task in time order before showing a proposed feature.
  • Separate quotations, recollections, verifiable materials, observations, and team interpretations; one interview cannot estimate population prevalence.
  • Use an unverified bottleneck to design research that could overturn the proposal instead of treating polite agreement as approval to build.

A user saying “I’d use this” is a reason to continue research, not enough evidence on its own to approve a feature. A more informative interview starts with the last task: what triggered it, what the participant did, where they paused or repeated work, and what finally allowed them to finish. Then separate the participant’s recollection from materials that can be checked and from the team’s interpretation before choosing the next step that could confirm or disprove the product hypothesis. This article uses enterprise expense reimbursement as an explicitly hypothetical scenario. No real users were interviewed, and no prevalence or conversion data was collected.

State which decision the interview should change

Before the session, write down the decision the team is uncertain about. For example, is the question “What are the main reasons reimbursement submissions are returned?” or “Can automatic classification reduce the total handling time?” The first requires understanding different causes in a workflow. The second also requires a definition of completion and a way to observe time. Without a clear question, a team can mistake “users like the idea” for an answer without explaining what that answer changes.

The service manual’s guide to capturing research questions recommends capturing and revisiting questions across development phases such as discovery, alpha, and beta. It also distinguishes questions the team wants research to answer from the questions it asks a participant. An internal question might be “Which steps cause resubmission?” A participant-facing prompt could be “What happened the last time you submitted?” Group and prioritize the decision questions first, then select an interview, observation, or prototype activity. That sequence is less likely to turn a proposed product solution into a leading question. Recruitment counts and session duration depend on the research question, user groups, and available resources. Numbers in the service manual are advice for its service-research context, not statistical sampling laws for every product.

Reconstruct an event in time order

Do not start with “Would automatic classification make this easier?” That question supplies the solution and makes polite agreement the simplest response. Ask for a specific recent experience instead: roughly when it happened, what triggered it, what work the person was trying to complete, what they did first, what happened next, where they had to pause, and how they eventually finished. When a vague term appears, ask what it meant in that event: “When you say it was returned, was that a request for more material or an approver's rejection?” “What did you do after that?” Avoid naming the problem for the participant.

For interview technique, the in-depth interview guide recommends open, neutral questions grounded in stories and real examples, rather than discussion of what should happen. It also covers informed consent, explaining research and recording, and securely handling personal data. A discussion guide helps interviewers cover consistent topics, but it is not a rigid script; following details a participant has just mentioned can be more useful than finishing every prompt in order. Listening to the existing workflow before showing a prototype helps reduce the influence of the proposed solution on later recall. If the research objective is prototype evaluation, place the task observation after the discussion of prior experience and record the two parts separately.

In a hypothetical expense workflow, the same sentence—“My last claim was returned”—could describe at least three different paths. First, an employee chose the wrong expense category and an approver asked them to select another; automatic classification might help, but the team still needs to know whether the rule is stable and what a wrong classification would cost. Second, ownership of the expense across projects was unclear; even a perfect “travel expense” label would not determine the project. The problem may be showing the allocation rule at submission time. Third, an image or required invoice field failed validation; the need may be better material checks or clearer correction guidance. Merging all three into “reimbursement is frustrating” encourages a team to choose automatic classification before discovering which task is difficult.

Record the event as a sequence of trigger, participants, inputs, actions, pauses, errors, recovery, and outcome. At each point ask what the person did, how they chose, whether they asked someone for help, and what workaround they used. If the participant volunteers redacted material, it may clarify fields and sequence; reluctance to share may reflect sensitive financial or colleague information and should not be treated as a failure to cooperate. Materials are one possible source of evidence, not a condition for every interview. To verify how often a process is returned, the team needs authorized operational data with a suitable definition; one recollection is not a system log.

Preserve the evidence layers in the notes

Research notes can combine at least five different things: a participant's exact words, their recollection of a past event, what the researcher observed in the session, material that can be checked, and the team's interpretation. These are related but not equivalent. “It was returned twice last time” is a recollection. If the participant consents to show redacted records, they may corroborate a return time and reason. “Classification is the main bottleneck” is a team interpretation. A quotation does not automatically prove prevalence, and observing one task does not represent every user group.

Keep a session identifier, topic, event sequence, material type, and essential context, while removing names, account details, receipt numbers, and unnecessary personal information. Do not rewrite a researcher's summary as a quotation from a participant. The GOV.UK analysis guide recommends recording what observers actually saw or heard separately from findings and actions. That makes it possible to return to the source and ask how a conclusion was reached. If several observers independently write down observations before sorting them together, the group is also less likely to let one researcher's immediate explanation become the only account of what happened.

An initial, falsifiable hypothesis could be: “If unclear expense ownership is the main obstacle, showing the project-allocation rule before submission should reduce returns more than adding automatic category recognition.” The statement names a possible cause, an intervention, and a prediction that differs from an alternative. In contrast, if the evidence points to several kinds of material-validation failures while allocation is clear, the hypothesis should change. Preserve both supporting and contradictory clues instead of selecting only the statements that fit a launch proposal.

What one interview cannot settle

An interview can help a team understand how one person experienced a task, the language they use for a problem, and where they needed an improvised step. It cannot establish that every user has the same problem or determine market size by itself. Volunteers, manager-referred participants, and employees from one department may not represent other groups. Record how participants were recruited and which people or contexts the research did not include so the team can understand the limits of its interpretation.

The guide to researching user experiences describes event cards and experience maps. Participants reconstruct events in time order and distinguish what happened from what they wish had happened. For its particular research activity, the guide suggests recruiting at least four people per distinct user type when types have been identified, or eight people when they have not. Those figures are operational guidance for that method, not a guarantee that eight participants represent a population. Estimating prevalence still requires a defined target population, sampling approach, and appropriate quantitative evidence.

Close the interview by choosing the next research activity that matches the unresolved question. If the question is how a process actually works, ask a participant to complete a dedicated test task while explaining their thinking, or observe an authorized real workflow. If the question is whether a proposal is understandable, run a prototype task and observe completion rather than asking only whether the person likes the design. These activities answer different questions. A design demonstration should not double as proof of need, usability, and market demand.

Do not turn observations into findings immediately after the session. The service manual’s research analysis guide recommends involving observers soon after a research round, first recording what they saw or heard, grouping observations by themes or journey stage, and only then writing findings and actions at separate levels. For the hypothetical example, a useful output might be three possible return mechanisms, the source for each, an unknown rate, a counterexample that could overturn automatic classification, and a question for the next research round. Such an output is more useful than “users need smart reimbursement” because the team can see what is known, what is inferred, and what remains unanswered.

A launch gate need not say “someone in an interview expressed interest.” A team can require a valuable task outcome, an observable obstacle affecting that outcome, a description of which users and contexts are covered, an alternative explanation, and a statement of what observation would change the proposal or stop further investment. Treat this as a suggested decision framework, not an industry standard. Discuss schedule and investment only after the outcome and time cost can be assessed.

Treat “I’d use it” as an entry to the conversation rather than its conclusion. Start with a recent task: trace the trigger, steps, and workaround, keep words, recollections, materials, observations, and interpretations separate, and ask a more testable question. When the evidence is insufficient, record “not yet known.” Before scheduling development, connect the research question to the evidence that could change the decision and to later acceptance criteria in requirements and acceptance review.
