Users Say They Would Use It: Ask About the Last Real Task First

Use recent task evidence to test product assumptions without confusing polite agreement with demand.

M Midnight Engineering notes on development, quality, security, delivery, and operations. View Author Profile
Updated 2026-10-09 · Estimated read 7 min
Translations
zh-CN en

Article

A user saying “I would use this” is a reason to ask another question, not sufficient evidence to approve a feature. Discovery needs a recent task: what triggered it, how it was completed, where it stalled, and what workaround made completion possible. Expense reimbursement is a hypothetical example here. No interviews, sample, or conversion results were collected for this article.

Reconstruct an event before presenting a solution

“Would automatic classification make reimbursement easier?” suggests its own answer. Instead, ask about the last claim: where receipts came from, who chose the expense category, and who returned the submission. Follow the timeline before showing the proposed feature.

The GOV.UK interview guide recommends open, neutral questions grounded in stories and real examples. It also addresses informed consent and secure storage of personal data. These principles do not make its recruitment practices a ready-made sampling design for an enterprise product.

The obstacle might be unclear cost ownership or an unsupported invoice format. Automatic classification would address different amounts of either problem. Labeling both “reimbursement is frustrating” hides the distinction that should shape a prototype.

Keep quotations, recollections, and interpretations separate

A quotation records what someone said. Their account remains a recollection unless supported by evidence; retain a session or material reference while removing identifying details. “Classification is the main bottleneck” is the team’s interpretation. Preserve the provenance of each rather than polishing them into one apparently certain conclusion.

“Returned twice last time” does not mean “always returned.” Ask why and what happened next. With consent, inspect redacted material. Refusal may reflect sensitive financial or colleague information; record the limitation rather than demanding evidence.

A useful hypothesis would be: if unclear ownership causes returns, explaining ownership before submission may deserve testing before automatic classification. That is a proposal, not a demonstrated outcome. Define observable task completion before choosing a prototype or further research.

What interviews cannot settle alone

Volunteers may disproportionately represent dissatisfied users; manager-selected participants may omit other groups. A recollection can reveal a process but cannot establish prevalence. Estimating coverage requires an explicit sample and measurement definition.

Should a feature demonstration come first? Hear the existing workflow first when discovery is the objective. Use task observation when evaluating a prototype. If both happen together, preserve their order because the demonstration may influence later recollections.

For this example, a sourced task sequence, an unverified bottleneck, and a counterexample that could overturn the proposal are useful deliverables. Bring them into requirements and acceptance review and decide what evidence would change the investment decision before scheduling implementation.
