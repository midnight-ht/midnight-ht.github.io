Who sets priority and accepts handoff during an incident?

Separate incident command, system operations, communications, and planning as explicit responsibilities; coordinate parallel response with a live state and acknowledged handoff.

M Midnight Engineering notes on development, quality, security, delivery, and operations. View Author Profile
Updated 2026-11-25 · Estimated read 19 min
Translations
zh-CN en

Article

Key takeaways

  • Command assigns priority and work; operations changes the system and reports results.
  • Roles may be combined; request support or hand off explicitly when workload exceeds capacity.
  • A handoff carries active work, evidence, and the next decision time, then gets the new owner's acknowledgement.

When two engineers are preparing production changes at once, the first incident question is who sets their order, who executes each change, and what evidence will show whether it worked. Incident command is not a more senior fixer; it organizes priority, assignments, and current state. The response also needs someone to communicate known impact and someone to track follow-up. A small team can combine roles, but responsibility should not disappear in the rush.

Separate impact mitigation from response management

The Google SRE Workbook describes resolving an incident as mitigating impact or restoring service. Managing an incident means coordinating responders and keeping information flowing within the team and to interested parties. These tasks happen at the same time, but they are not the same job. An operations engineer may be testing whether a rollback reduces errors. Meanwhile, a commander assesses impact, decides which teams are needed, chooses the order of mitigations, and defines what evidence is needed before the next decision. If the operator must also coordinate everything, the technical task can consume their attention.

Chapter 14 of the Google SRE Book adapts the Incident Command System into command, operations, communications, and planning. The source describes practice at a particular organization and scale; it does not require every company to copy its titles. It offers boundaries: command holds high-level state and delegates by need and priority; operations applies tools to the system; communications updates responders and stakeholders; planning tracks longer-running issues, follow-up work, and handoffs. The chapter's Google practice even specifies that the operations group should be the only group modifying the system during an incident. That is an explicit change-control boundary; whether to adopt the same strict rule depends on a local authorization model.

The roles should reduce guesswork, not create ceremony. The operations lead knows who can authorize the next change. Command knows what is in flight. Communications does not publish an unverified hypothesis as root cause. When two objectives compete, responders know who decides the order instead of letting the last person to speak decide implicitly.

A hypothetical conflict between parallel fixes

Suppose API errors rise. One engineer prepares to roll back the latest release, while another suspects a configuration change and wants to adjust a traffic switch. If both alter production without a shared state, the second action can overwrite the first action's observation window. Even if errors fall, the team may not know which change helped; if the switch sends traffic to an overloaded area, impact may grow. That imagined case illustrates coordination responsibility. It is not an incident this site experienced, and it does not imply that concurrent actions always cause harm.

The commander can break the decision into short, recordable steps: establish current user impact and baseline; choose one mitigation that reduces impact, has bounded risk, and can be reversed; name the operator and observation signal; record the action time and expected result; then decide what to do after the result returns. Other engineers can run read-only investigations or work on independent tasks with explicit boundaries. The operator reports what changed and what was observed; a planned action is not the same as a recovered service.

Priority should account for impact, reversibility, risk, and observability rather than job title or volume of speech. Mitigation may take priority over root-cause analysis, but if a mitigation could overload another service, command must consider the spillover. Elsewhere in the chapter, an uncoordinated production change to show how a well-intentioned fix can worsen an incident. That supports coordinated changes; it does not prove that every team needs one commander to approve every command. The organization should set permission granularity according to change risk.

Combining roles does not erase responsibility

A low-complexity event may need one on-call responder; mechanically splitting four roles can add communication overhead. The real question is whether one person can actually carry command and operations at the same time. If mitigation requires watching logs, changing systems, and observing results, that person may not also maintain the state record, answer every inquiry, and plan a handoff. A team can start with combined roles and define escalation triggers: impact expands, another team is needed, concurrent changes increase, stakeholder updates become frequent, or the current owner cannot refresh the overall state within an agreed interval.

The Workbook describes communications being subsumed back into command as an incident shrinks. Roles can adapt to workload; the team does not need a fixed headcount for the whole event. But every merge or split should be reflected in the state record so responders know who owns each responsibility. When a role is overloaded, command can ask the planning lead for more staff; in a small team, the equivalent is to name a successor and obtain acknowledgement.

Combined roles also call for an independent check. If the commander is performing a high-risk operation, another person should review the plan, record the command, and observe the result where possible. If nobody is available, choose a more conservative, reversible action and record that limitation. This article does not define an automatic approval policy for any system or report a live production exercise. Actual permission and review boundaries belong to the service's change-control rules.

A state document is not meeting minutes

The Google SRE Book recommends a live incident state document that multiple people can update and retain for later analysis. Its job is not to transcribe every chat message. A responder taking over should quickly be able to answer operational questions: What is the current impact? Which facts were observed, and which are hypotheses? What is in flight? Who owns it? When will the result arrive? What evidence is needed for the next decision? Put the most important information where it is easy to find; a detailed log without decision state is still hard to use.

For example, a record might say: “Operator A rolled back at 14:05 from release r42 to r41; errors fell from 8% to 2% during a five-minute observation window; the mobile login path remains unverified; next decision at 14:15.” In an actual incident, the entry should link to the query or change record. If values are illustrative, label them hypothetical. This record lets command distinguish “tried and ineffective” from “not yet executed,” preventing another engineer from unknowingly repeating the same action.

A communications lead's update should state known impact, mitigations already taken, unresolved questions, and the next update time. Do not turn an investigative hypothesis into an ETA promise just to answer a question. Periodic updates belong to the communications role in the book; update frequency, channels, and public wording still require local policy. This article sends no incident messages and has no site incident to report.

A handoff needs acceptance and a next step

A handoff is not a link to the chat. The outgoing owner should transfer current impact, active operations, unverified results, risks, evidence locations, and the next decision condition. The incoming owner should confirm that they accept command or operations responsibility. The chapter explicitly recommends an acknowledged handoff once the new commander is fully briefed, and telling the other responders who is now in charge. Without that step, the outgoing owner may believe the work has transferred while the incoming person thinks they are only observing.

A concise handoff might say: “I am transferring incident command to B. Rollback observation is active; at 14:15 check error rate and login success. Edge-cache impact is still unverified. B, please confirm you accept command.” Then update the state document with the new owner and time. If there is no response, the outgoing commander retains responsibility or escalates according to the pre-agreed process; silence is not acceptance. A shift overlap should be long enough to transfer context, rather than waiting until the previous owner is offline to discover the new one never received it.

Incident closure also needs evidence. An alert clearing may mean only that one signal changed; service health should be checked along critical user paths and an observation window. Mitigation does not mean the root cause is known. Resuming a rollout or removing safeguards before understanding the cause needs its own justification. A proposed tabletop exercise can introduce two competing mitigations, a customer-impact update, an overloaded command role, and a shift handoff; then check who sets priority, whether changes are recorded, how results return, what the stakeholder update says, and whether the handoff is accepted. The exercise is proposed rather than performed here; no response-time improvement is claimed. For verifiable follow-through after an incident, see postmortem action acceptance.
