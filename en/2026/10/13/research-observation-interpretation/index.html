Do Not Write an Action as Its Cause: Use Competing Explanations to Choose the Next Research Step

A user returning to the previous page is an observation, not proof that a form is confusing. Separate behavior, cause hypotheses and actions, then choose research that can distinguish them.

Article

For example, “the participant opened the delivery-address page, did not submit, and returned to the order” records a visible action; “the user does not understand the address form” has already chosen a cause. Research notes should keep observation, interpretation and action separate, while retaining the source, alternatives and next evidence that could change the view. Otherwise, a concise sentence can become a supposed user fact as it moves through reviews and planning.

Record an event that can be revisited

The GOV.UK guide to analysing a research session recommends recording what participants actually said or did before sorting observations, forming findings and deciding actions. It also recommends one observation per note and using recordings, when available, to confirm an observation or verbatim quote. A separate guide to notes and recordings distinguishes observations from an observer's personal interpretation. The address flow below is hypothetical; this site did not conduct the research described.

Suppose a participant is completing an order. They open the delivery-address page, pause without submitting, then return to the order. A traceable observation could read: “The task asked the participant to confirm delivery details; they opened the address page, did not submit, and returned to review the items.” If the research notes contain a timestamp, attach the recording location. If there is no reliable timestamp, do not add a precise-looking number from memory. If the participant said something that can be located, quote it and label the session. A recollection that cannot be checked should be marked as an observer summary, not wrapped in quotation marks.

Task and page context matter. The same return action may mean something different before payment than after address confirmation; a note that only says “the user dropped out” does not show which step was involved or what happened before it. A participant and session reference can help the team return to the relevant material, but notes need not copy names, addresses or organizations just to look complete. For material handling, the GOV.UK notes and recordings guide notes that research materials may contain personal data: obtain informed consent before taking notes or recording, and store and share material according to the agreed purposes and security rules. Traceability does not mean everyone should have access to raw recordings.

One action can support several explanations

The move from “returned to the order” to “the form is difficult” skips an inference. The participant might have wanted to check which address was selected, verify the items or delivery cost, missed a continue button, or been interrupted. Each explanation points toward a different response: make the selected address clearer, explain delivery cost, change the button, or pause the design change and gather more evidence. When the explanation is embedded in the observation sentence, the team cannot see those branches.

Keep adjacent but distinct fields: the observed event and its source; the current interpretation; alternatives that remain plausible; evidence for and against each interpretation; and the next action that can distinguish them. For example, if the same hypothetical session included the participant saying, “I want to check whether this goes to work or home,” that quote would support the explanation that the selected address is unclear. Without the quote, do not insert it as if the participant said it. Several colleagues reviewing the same recording can identify omissions and challenge each other's bias, but their agreement about one recording does not increase the number of participants.

This also changes how a “finding” is written. GOV.UK presents grouping observations, forming a concise finding and deciding an action as separate analysis steps. Analysts can group related notes, but should explain the basis for grouping and preserve counterexamples that do not fit the current theme. One participant returning once is a lead for another research question; it does not support a claim such as “most users abandon the address step.” When observations come from different tasks, pages or product versions, similar-looking actions alone do not prove the same cause.

Choose a next step that distinguishes hypotheses

An action does not have to be “redesign the form now.” A more useful action makes competing explanations predict different things. In the hypothetical scenario, a follow-up session could ask neutrally what the participant intended to find, then invite them to recall the order of their checks. Avoid “Did the address selection confuse you?” That question supplies the team's diagnosis; agreement no longer distinguishes an existing thought from an answer shaped by the prompt.

Another option is a prototype that changes only the presentation of the selected address while leaving the other copy, button and item details alone. If participants identify the current address sooner and no longer need to return to the order, that would make the “default information was not clear” explanation more plausible. If they still return, investigate another cause rather than discarding the inconvenient observation. This kind of comparison does not prove causality by itself: task order, participant background, familiarity and other differences can affect behavior. Record what changed, what remained the same, and which explanation the observation supports or weakens.

Choose an interview, a review of existing material or a prototype test according to what remains unknown. If the missing information is the participant's goal at that moment, ask about the concrete event. If the disagreement is whether a default address is visible, test a prototype that changes that one feature. If the return came from analytics without task context, first reconstruct the event and version. No method automatically removes bias, and a small test cannot estimate what proportion of all users behave that way.

Hand off the finding without hiding uncertainty

A finding that can guide planning should tell the next person which session it came from, the task and interface at the time, the current interpretation, what has not been ruled out, who will check the next step, and what result would change the decision. For a research handover, the GOV.UK guide to sharing findings also recommends separating research context, findings and next steps so that people who were absent can understand where the finding came from and what the team intends to do. One possible record could be: “Observation O-12 → hypotheses H-2 (selected address unclear) / H-3 (checking items) → follow-up question Q-4 → decision D-7.” These identifiers are a proposed way to maintain traceability, not a process implemented on this site.

An action may be more research, revised wording in a report, a pause in feature work, or a design change after the evidence is stronger. Not every observation needs to become a development ticket. When the explanation is unsettled, a small, falsifiable hypothesis makes the review point and rollback condition easier to agree on than a bundle of page changes. A decision not to change the product can also be a decision, provided its uncertainty and the condition for revisiting it are explicit.

When should a note stay incomplete?

If nobody has reviewed the recording, a quote cannot be located, an observer's memory is uncertain, or the participant/session link is unreliable, state the evidence gap instead of turning it into a smooth narrative. Several second-hand summaries may all originate from one meeting and should not be counted as independent user evidence. If material contains personal information, do not widen access just to give the team a feeling of hearing the “raw voice”; follow the consent and data-access rules before deciding how to share it.

The address-flow event, quote and prototype outcomes in this article are hypothetical. No recording was reviewed, no participant interviewed and no prototype tested for this article. It explains how to organize evidence and the next decision, not a research result from this site. For designing questions around concrete events, see interviewing around recent events; for deciding when discovery has enough evidence to stop, see discovery stop criteria.
