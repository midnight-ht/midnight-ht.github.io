What Can Still Happen After You Configure a PDB?

A PodDisruptionBudget constrains voluntary evictions through the Eviction API. Node failures, direct deletion, and controller rollouts have different boundaries.

M Midnight Engineering notes on development, quality, security, delivery, and operations. View Author Profile
Updated 2026-11-25 · Estimated read 18 min
Translations
zh-CN en

Article

Key takeaways

  • A PDB protects particular voluntary evictions through the Eviction API; it cannot prevent involuntary failures and direct deletion bypasses it.
  • When drain waits on a budget, inspect selectors, available replicas, replacement scheduling, and unhealthy-Pod policy before considering an exception.
  • Percentage budgets round up. Calculate the actual replica count and validate capacity, readiness, and rollout settings separately.

A PodDisruptionBudget (PDB) constrains particular voluntary evictions made through the Kubernetes Eviction API. A node can still fail, a Pod can still be deleted directly, and a Deployment can still follow its own rollout pace. The practical question is not whether replicas can still disappear. It is which actors use the protected API, which Pods the budget counts, and whether the remaining replicas can actually keep serving.

A PDB governs a request path, not every outcome

Kubernetes distinguishes voluntary from involuntary disruptions. Hardware failure, a kernel panic, a node network partition, and eviction caused by node resource pressure can make Pods unavailable. The budget cannot prevent those involuntary events, although they count against it. When a maintenance actor requests removal through the Eviction API, the API checks whether that voluntary disruption is currently permitted. Node draining commonly uses this path: an eviction can be rejected temporarily, and the tool may retry until the request succeeds or a timeout is reached.

A PDB is therefore better understood as an admission gate for certain maintenance actions than as armor around replicas. If a node holding a replica suddenly disappears, the budget will not move that Pod back or undo the failure. It can only make a later, budget-respecting eviction request account for current availability. Conversely, deleting a Pod or its Deployment directly bypasses the PDB. Whether a human command, provider maintenance tool, or automation respects the budget depends on whether it calls the Eviction API. The existence of a PDB object does not prove that every deletion path is protected.

The official documentation marks PDBs stable since Kubernetes v1.21. This article checked the current Kubernetes documentation pages on November 25, 2026. No cluster was accessed, and no drain or deletion was run. Confirm the actual cluster version, hosting platform, and maintenance tool behavior separately.

Follow the budget through one node maintenance operation

Suppose a Deployment has three desired replicas spread across three nodes, and its PDB sets minAvailable: 2. These numbers illustrate the mechanism; they are not a capacity measurement. When an operator first drains one node, the Eviction API can allow one Pod to enter termination while the other two remain available. The budget’s remaining allowance falls. The controller creates a replacement, but a replacement that is not yet Ready does not count as available. If the operator requests eviction from another node before the replacement becomes available, that second request may be rejected because it would leave fewer than two available replicas.

Retrying does not mean the cluster has enough capacity to finish maintenance. If the scheduler cannot place the replacement, it may remain Pending and the budget may not recover; drain can eventually time out. “The budget stopped another eviction” is then an expected signal. Investigate allocatable node capacity, Pod requests, affinity and topology constraints, volume binding, and readiness before deciding whether to add capacity, lower maintenance concurrency, or authorize a documented exception. Removing the PDB only removes that gate. Removing the budget gives a Pending Pod neither a node nor additional service capacity.

The outcome may differ if a replica was already unhealthy before maintenance began. The object cannot repair that failure; current availability still affects its allowance. For eviction of unhealthy Pods during a node drain, the default policy can wait for the application Pod to become healthy. The documentation recommends considering unhealthyPodEvictionPolicy: AlwaysAllow so a drain can evict a misbehaving Pod. That choice permits maintenance to proceed with an unhealthy Pod; it is not a recovery mechanism or a risk-free switch. Evaluate whether the service can tolerate fewer replicas, how long the failure has lasted, and what recovery path exists.

A minAvailable value does not measure service capacity

Its arithmetic uses Pod availability and the desired replica count of the managing workload. That count says nothing about different replica throughput, whether traffic reaches the expected Service endpoints, whether several Pods share a rack, or whether a database dependency is already overloaded. Three Ready Pods may still share one failure domain. Two remaining Pods may be unable to carry current traffic because of connection-pool limits, CPU pressure, or downstream rate limiting. The budget answers whether Kubernetes may accept a protected eviction request; it cannot establish whether the application’s service level will still be met.

Integer and percentage settings also create different boundaries. The official configuration guide rounds percentages up: with seven desired replicas, minAvailable: "50%" requires at least four to remain; maxUnavailable: "30%" is also rounded up. A percentage is therefore not always an exact or conservative fraction. With only one desired replica, maxUnavailable: "30%" can still permit that one replica to be disrupted, producing 100% unavailability. For small or quorum-based services, substitute the actual replica count and record the resulting integer instead of reviewing only the percentage string in YAML.

This is why replica placement, probe semantics, and capacity validation belong beside PDB design. Anti-affinity or topology spread reduces the chance that replicas fail together. Readiness determines which Pods are considered eligible to receive traffic. Load evidence helps establish whether the remaining instances can serve. These controls complement one another, but none is implied by a PDB field. If readiness is too permissive, the budget may count Pods that cannot handle requests. If it is too strict, maintenance can be stalled for a long time. Validating whether a probe reflects service ability requires representative traffic or test evidence, not just YAML inspection.

Why rollouts need their own availability settings

Unavailability during an application rollout is reflected in budget-related state, but a Deployment or StatefulSet is not directly constrained by the PDB while performing its own rolling update. The workload resource’s update strategy controls how failures during that update are handled. The PDB may show a consumed allowance while the workload controller continues replacing Pods according to its own policy. During a release, review maxUnavailable, maxSurge, readiness gates, and the progress deadline separately, and check them against startup time and service capacity. Treating a PDB as the Deployment’s rollout gate creates a false safety assumption.

Termination has another boundary. Eviction through the API honors the Pod’s terminationGracePeriodSeconds, but a grace period only gives a process time to exit. That interval alone cannot prove that the process drained connections or completed in-flight work; traffic switching remains a separate concern. See application shutdown and platform termination budgets for that separate mechanism.

What should be validated before maintenance?

First identify the controller for the replicas, the Pods actually matched by the PDB selector, the desired replica count, and current availability. Then verify that the maintenance tool calls the Eviction API. In a test environment or controlled maintenance window, check how a single allowed eviction changes the budget; whether a request is rejected when a replica is already unavailable; how drain waits and times out when a replacement cannot be scheduled for lack of resources; and what changes when the unhealthy-Pod policy changes. Record cluster and tool versions, API or event evidence for each step, and the conditions for recovery. These are proposed checks, not operations performed for this article.

When a budget blocks maintenance for a long time, distinguish a selector mismatch from genuinely low availability, an unschedulable replacement, or a policy that is waiting on an unhealthy Pod. Address the cause indicated by the evidence. If the budget must be bypassed temporarily, document who approved it, which constraint is being removed, the expected service risk, the signals to watch, and when the protection will be restored. Independent service-side safeguards should already be understood. A PDB connects a specific maintenance request to current availability; it cannot replace fault-domain design, rollout policy, capacity planning, monitoring, or application-level resilience.
