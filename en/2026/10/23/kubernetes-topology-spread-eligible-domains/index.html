Kubernetes Topology Spread: Calculate Eligible Domains Before maxSkew

Use a two-domain calculation to distinguish minDomains, hard skew, soft preference, and real capacity.

M Midnight Engineering notes on development, quality, security, delivery, and operations. View Author Profile
Updated 2026-10-23 · Estimated read 14 min
Translations
zh-CN en

Article

Key takeaways

  • Define counted Pods and eligible domains first.
  • Too few eligible domains make the minimum zero.
  • Soft preference does not remove hard constraints.

A cluster has three labeled availability zones, yet a new replica remains Pending. Do not begin by counting three regions on a map. Kubernetes topology spreading first determines which domains participate, then evaluates skew at a candidate placement. maxSkew: 1 neither requires one replica in every zone nor creates a node that satisfies other scheduling constraints.

Which domains supply the minimum?

The Kubernetes topology spread reference defines hard skew relative to the target domain and a global minimum. If eligible domains fall below minDomains, that minimum becomes zero. ScheduleAnyway instead makes reducing skew a scheduling preference. The following examples are paper calculations, not executed cluster experiments.

A domain is defined by a topology-label key and value: nodes sharing one topology.kubernetes.io/zone value form a zone domain. A Pod label selector defines which existing Pods count. A labeled zone may be excluded by the applicable node eligibility rules, while a Pod in an included zone may not match the count selector. Write down both the counted population and the participating domains before discussing balance. Node counts are not replica counts.

Counting eligibility also differs from final placement feasibility. nodeAffinityPolicy and nodeTaintsPolicy determine how certain node conditions affect the spreading calculation; they do not remove affinity, toleration, or resource requirements from scheduling. A domain can influence the minimum while lacking a node that satisfies every requirement of the incoming Pod. Being counted is not a promise that the scheduler can place work there. Check optional-field availability and feature status against the actual cluster version rather than assuming the live documentation describes every older environment.

Calculate the same placement twice

Suppose zones A, B, and C originally hold one matching replica each. Zone C no longer satisfies this Pod’s node selection and is excluded under the eligibility policies chosen for this example. A and B remain eligible. The incoming Pod uses DoNotSchedule, maxSkew: 1, and an explicit minDomains: 3. Other constraints are temporarily set aside to isolate this calculation; none of these numbers comes from real scheduler logs.

Make the counting contract explicit: existing counted Pods and the incoming Pod carry app=orders, and labelSelector selects that label. Set nodeAffinityPolicy: Honor; the incoming Pod’s node selection accepts only A and B, excluding C from the eligible-domain set. Assume no taint differences that change eligibility. The incoming Pod therefore contributes to the selected population, and the exclusion of C follows a stated policy.

Two eligible domains are fewer than the required three, so the baseline is zero rather than the smallest existing count of one. Placing the incoming Pod in A produces a count of two, making its skew from zero equal to two. That exceeds one. B produces the same result. The constraint blocks further concentration in the remaining zones. The policy preserves the team’s fault-distribution requirement at the cost of a waiting replica; it is not evidence that the scheduler overlooked remaining capacity.

For comparison, keep one matching Pod in each of A and B but remove the requirement for three eligible domains. If the effective minimum-domain requirement is now satisfied, the global minimum is one. Placement in A raises its count to two and produces skew one, which this constraint permits. Resource, affinity, and other filters still apply. The two calculations differ because eligibility and the baseline differ, not because maxSkew sometimes stops working.

Choosing ScheduleAnyway changes the hard rejection into a preference. Other hard requirements remain, and even a soft spreading preference cannot guarantee equal load. Operations and product owners must choose whether reduced-zone capacity should leave some replicas waiting or permit more concentration in surviving zones. Making Pending disappear is only one consequence of relaxing the policy.

Two ways to count the wrong population

A mismatched selector can invalidate the paper calculation. If both old and new versions carry app=orders, a constraint selecting only that label can count both versions. A team intending per-version spreading must examine its selector and relevant version support instead of substituting only the new deployment’s replica list. Narrowing a selector merely to unblock one rollout can change the fault distribution during coexistence.

Missing topology labels are another trap. A node residing in a cloud zone does not establish that Kubernetes has the expected label. Inspect actual keys and values and confirm each intended domain appears in the scheduler’s view. Autoscaling also needs to understand the node groups implied by the constraints. Adding a node that still fails the selection requirements does not restore a missing eligible domain.

Will old replicas move when the third zone returns?

Do not infer automatic rebalancing from this scheduling constraint. It governs placement of incoming Pods; existing replicas do not automatically move merely because a domain returns. Restoring distribution may require a separately controlled replacement or rebalancing process, with capacity, disruption, and business impact reviewed. Redistributing replicas and maintaining service availability are separate observed outcomes.

Validate in stages. Record the actual Pod constraint, count selector, node labels, eligibility policies, and matching population; calculate expected candidates before scheduling. In a controlled environment, remove a domain, restore it, and change matching labels while observing events and incoming placements. If events also report insufficient resources, distinguish resource filtering from skew rejection instead of blaming every Pending Pod on one field.

Replica counts and business traffic distribution are separate. Two lightly loaded replicas in A and one long-running worker in B can satisfy a count-based condition without equal work or equal failure impact. Capacity planning still needs node sizes, traffic entry, and application processing behavior. If acceptance requires surviving a zone loss while taking orders, verify task outcomes in surviving zones. Satisfying a skew calculation does not establish a service-level objective.

Scaling, zone failure, and rolling updates change counts at different times. A screenshot cannot establish persistent fault distribution. Record object revisions and observation times to explain how old and new replicas influence each decision. The earlier discussion of Pod termination budgets addresses capacity changes during exit. A useful spreading conclusion names the conditions and the incoming placements allowed or blocked, then checks business capacity separately.
