Low CPU, failed users: connect service indicators to incident learning

Distinguish accepted requests from completed jobs, define indicator populations, and make incident actions verifiable.

M Midnight Engineering notes on development, quality, security, delivery, and operations. View Author Profile
Updated 2026-10-08 · Estimated read 10 min
Translations
zh-CN en

Article

An asynchronous-task SLO should measure whether users obtain usable results on time, not merely whether the interface accepted requests. Define valid requests, deadlines, and observation windows before calculating success. Unfinished work needs explicit accounting, while alerts and incident actions need verifiable responses.

CPU, memory, and HTTP success can look healthy while users never receive an export. If job creation returns immediately, a stopped worker may leave request-level dashboards looking reassuring.

For this hypothetical asynchronous service, consider the user’s end result. Users need a usable, correct file; indicators should stay close to that outcome. Resource measurements help explain behavior but cannot establish task completion.

Define the population before the target

Google SRE’s service objectives chapter distinguishes a measured service indicator from the objective set for it. Choose measurements around behavior users care about.

For the proposed indicator, consider the fraction of valid export requests producing a usable file within an agreed time. Define whether capacity violations count as valid requests, how cancellations are handled, and whether an incorrect file can ever count as success. Changing these definitions can improve a chart without improving service.

With a hypothetical 10,000 valid requests and a 99.5% target, 50 requests may fail the condition. This is arithmetic for illustration, not site traffic or a recommended target. Payment systems and infrequent internal reports have different consequences and expectations.

Include unfinished work

Trace queue time, execution time, failure category, and artifact status by task identity. Unfinished work needs an accounting rule.

One proposed design assigns requests to a cohort by creation time, with a completion deadline for each task. Finalize the cohort after its last task’s deadline. A task still unfinished at its deadline counts once as a failed condition; eventual completion does not turn it into an on-time success. Each task belongs to one creation cohort, preventing duplicate counts across windows. Tasks whose deadlines have not arrived feed queue-age monitoring rather than a finalized SLO failure count.

The cohort rule is an illustrative design, not a universal definition. Agree on deadlines, valid requests, and cancellation handling. Record definition changes and their effective dates before comparing windows.

An alert should suggest an action: inspect a dependency, pause a task type, or contact an owner. Success-rate monitoring also cannot replace targeted checks for rare authorization failures.

Make incident actions testable

For a hypothetical stopped consumer, distinguish incident onset, first user impact, detection, response, and recovery. Preserve unknown timestamps rather than inventing a complete timeline.

“Add monitoring” is not an acceptance criterion. A stronger action names the owner and verifies that stopping a consumer in a test environment triggers the agreed notification and that recovery instructions work. Set the threshold from business waiting tolerance, not an arbitrary number in a tutorial.

Bring those actions into the development plan with a deadline and acceptance evidence. A future incident should let the team determine whether the promised change actually affected the failing path.

Connecting service and resource measurements

Are business indicators needed when resource dashboards exist?

They answer different questions. Resource and queue measurements help locate constraints; a business SLI describes whether users received the agreed service. A shrinking queue might indicate recovery or dropped jobs. Interpret it alongside results and integrity checks. Record waiting time, processing time, outcomes, and failure categories without treating one resource threshold as the user-success definition.

Does missing an SLO mean immediate rollback?

Not necessarily. Determine whether the release caused the change, whether the sample and indicator definitions are reliable, and whether rollback could worsen data inconsistency. Stopping exposure, pausing a task type, rolling back code, and recovering a dependency are different actions. Connect the decision to the stop conditions in the release and rollback plan.

Incident actions should return to requirements and testing. After adding deadline alerts, verify that stopping a consumer triggers notification and that recovery processes accumulated tasks as agreed. Feed that acceptance result into requirements and design instead of repeatedly reopening the same ownership question after each incident.
