Two pytest.approx tolerances are alternatives, not two mandatory gates

Choose a reference value, unit and error budget before approving the relative and absolute tolerance settings.

M Midnight Engineering notes on development, quality, security, delivery, and operations. View Author Profile
Updated 2026-10-18 · Estimated read 8 min
Translations
zh-CN en

Article

When pytest.approx receives both relative and absolute tolerances, satisfying either is enough. Reading those settings as two mandatory limits can disguise a wider acceptance range. Before making the assertion green, write down how much deviation is allowed and in which unit.

Defaults have a defined reference

The pytest approx reference specifies a default relative tolerance of one part per million of the expected value and an absolute tolerance of one trillionth. The relative comparison uses the expected value as its reference. It does not independently calculate a tolerance from each operand. Explicitly specifying abs without rel removes the default relative comparison; supplying both accepts either tolerance.

For a hypothetical expected value of one million units, a relative tolerance of one part per million permits one unit of deviation. Adding a much smaller absolute tolerance does not shrink that one-unit allowance to the smaller number. This is a paper calculation, not an executed pytest assertion or an endorsement of one unit for a particular business rule.

# Illustrative only; not executed.
assert actual == pytest.approx(expected, rel=1e-6, abs=1e-12)

Near zero, a purely relative quantity may poorly express the desired error budget, so an absolute tolerance needs a meaningful unit. Changing data from seconds to milliseconds changes the meaning of the same absolute number. Copying a small-looking parameter from another test does not establish an approved precision requirement.

Someone must own the acceptable deviation

I recommend recording the reference value’s source, measurement unit and reason for the allowance beside the test. An algorithm’s approximation budget, an instrument’s resolution and a business rounding rule may lead to different choices. These are proposed decision contexts. No algorithm or instrument was measured here, and no directly applicable numerical rule is offered for financial or other specialist domains.

Tolerance also cannot replace a required rounding procedure. If a business rule specifies rounding before comparison, test that rule. A loose approximation may accept an incorrect order of operations. When exact floating-point equality fails, distinguish representation effects, algorithmic error and an incorrect business result before deciding whether approximate equality belongs in the test.

Exercise the edge of the allowance

Proposed samples should fall inside and outside the allowance, approach zero and use substantially different expected magnitudes. One easy decimal case cannot explain behavior at a much larger scale. Values near an edge require attention to their actual representation; a decimal boundary on paper is not an executed exact result. Include negative values where the business conditions permit them rather than assuming positive-only samples suffice.

Treat non-finite values separately. The official reference says infinity equals only itself and NaN equals nothing by default; allowing NaN to compare with itself requires an explicit setting. Tool behavior does not decide whether missing numeric data is acceptable. If missing data should fail, do not broaden its meaning merely to make a collection pass.

None of these tests was executed. Preserve the original failure and the reason for changing the assertion, then require new cases to show that deviations outside the approved budget still fail. The October 13 article on parametrized object aliases addresses whether expectations remain independent from mutated input. What reviewers approve is an error range with a unit and reference value, rather than the bare fact that the test uses approx.
