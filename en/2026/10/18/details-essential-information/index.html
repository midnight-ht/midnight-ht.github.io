Use collapsed details for help, keep action conditions visible

Align conditions, supporting help, confirmation, and rule updates before registration commitment.

M Midnight Engineering notes on development, quality, security, delivery, and operations. View Author Profile
Updated 2026-10-24 · Estimated read 14 min
Translations
zh-CN en

Article

Key takeaways

  • Present critical conditions before the related decision.
  • Minority cases can need recognizable routes before irreversible actions.
  • Expansion counts do not prove understanding or completion.

A registration page can become shorter by moving its cancellation deadline behind “More information”, while withholding what users need to decide. Collapsed details suit supporting explanation, not critical conditions for the current action. Before choosing a component, identify whose choice the text changes and when. The fact that the system contains the text does not prove users encountered it before committing.

Separate decision conditions from supporting help

The GOV.UK details guidance recommends the component for information only some users need, advises against hiding information needed by most users, and calls for short descriptive entrances. This is component-selection guidance, not a legal conclusion about adequate disclosure. Conditions needed for a decision should be encountered when that decision is made.

The HTML Standard's details description defines a disclosure for additional information or controls, with summary providing its summary or legend. Native semantics express the disclosure relationship; they do not decide which business rule belongs in the main content. Correct elements and correct information hierarchy require separate judgments.

An editorial test used here temporarily covers the disclosure: could its absence reverse a judgment about eligibility, cost, or the next action's consequence? If so, consider promoting the relevant condition. This is a task-based recommendation without usability experiment results. It does not require every detail on the first screen; it requires encountering a condition before the decision it affects.

Put a cancellation deadline before commitment

Imagine a Saturday event with self-service cancellation available only until the end of Thursday, followed by manual handling. All dates and rules are hypothetical. The old page hides this behind “More information”. A user registers before learning that cancellation conflicts with their plans. The failure is missing information before commitment, not merely missing text on the confirmation page.

A candidate repair places an accurate short condition near registration, including the deadline and what happens afterward. Details retain the rationale, help locating a booking reference, and contact instructions. “Cancellation restrictions apply” is too vague if users must still expand it to know whether the condition matters. Conversely, dumping the entire internal process beside the button can bury the choice.

Continue the task: the user reads the condition, chooses to attend, enters details, reviews the event and deadline, and submits. Confirmation must agree with the earlier statement. It should not change self-service cancellation into a manual application without explanation. If rules change during entry, present the relevant change for confirmation rather than silently replacing the earlier promise with current server configuration.

A user unable to accept the deadline needs a way to leave or revise before submitting. Previously entered information should not become pressure to continue. If special arrangements are possible, provide a recognizable route with actual handling conditions. No real registration system was tested, and no reduction in mistakes is claimed. The example aligns information with the decision moment.

A minority's conditions may still deserve visibility

Population size alone is insufficient. A rule affecting few people can need prominent treatment when its consequences are difficult to undo. That is this article's product recommendation beyond the guidance's majority rule. Where the user's circumstances are known, show relevant information; otherwise make the applicable group recognizable without forcing everyone through all help content.

For example, most applicants may upload conventional evidence while some can supply only an alternative. Put normal formats and size limits near upload, and make the alternative route identifiable to the people who need it. Hiding it under “Other information” until submission fails assigns discovery effort to those already facing difficulty.

Requiring an expansion acknowledgment for every question is a counterexample. Users may learn to open and close each item without understanding it. Click logs do not prove comprehension. Explanation everyone needs belongs in main content; occasional help finding a document number can remain optional rather than becoming a mandatory ritual.

Does opening details by default replace hierarchy?

It changes initial visibility but does not settle organization. People may collapse the section, return later, or reach an action through another entrance. They still need accurate short conditions and a clear task relationship. If a critical rule uses an initially open disclosure merely to preserve a component's appearance, consider whether an ordinary paragraph or notice is clearer.

HTML's same-name details grouping supports exclusive opening, but the standard asks authors to consider effects on finding and comparing information. If cancellation and refund rules need to be considered together, opening one while closing the other adds a memory burden. Saving vertical space does not establish suitability. Examine whether users need both conditions together.

The entrance must describe its contents. “Help me find my booking reference” is more informative than “More”. If the content now explains refund eligibility, update the old help title. Recheck names after translation, rule changes, and component reuse. Accurate disclosure text does not excuse hiding critical conditions, but helps supporting information reach the people who need it.

Distinguish discovery, understanding, and completion

A proposed study asks users to make a decision from the default view, then explain their understood deadline and consequence. Separately observe whether those needing help find it. Test keyboard opening and closing, reading order, and target assistive technology. Record encountering information, understanding it, and completing the task rather than counting expansions alone. An unopened section does not necessarily mean misunderstanding; an opened one does not establish comprehension.

These checks have not been executed. As with the site's form question protocol, content needs an explainable purpose. Select one rule capable of changing a registration decision and compare its short condition, disclosure, confirmation, and update path. Collapsing content helps reading only when those parts make the same promise about the same decision.
