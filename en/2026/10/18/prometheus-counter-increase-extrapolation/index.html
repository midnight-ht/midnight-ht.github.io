Why Prometheus increase can be fractional for integer counters

Prometheus extrapolates counter increases over a query window. Interpret fractions, resets, and display precision against that measurement scope.

An integer counter can produce a fractional increase. The calculation adjusts counter resets and extrapolates the increase across the full requested time window. Before diagnosing broken instrumentation from a decimal on a dashboard, check the operation and sampling window. A non-integer result alone establishes neither duplicated requests nor a damaged counter.

A window estimate is not an event-by-event count

Prometheus’s increase documentation explicitly allows non-integer results even when a counter rises only in integer increments. The operation also adjusts breaks in monotonicity, such as resets after a target restart. It estimates an increase over a time range rather than counting rows from a collection of individual business events.

Suppose an application adds one for every completed request, monitoring scrapes snapshots on its own schedule, and a panel asks for the last five minutes. Scrapes need not fall exactly on the two window boundaries. The question is therefore more involved than subtracting two integer snapshots. This is a hypothetical explanation of sampling and windows, with no real scrape series or executed query. No precise decimal is fabricated as an observed result.

A decimal demonstrates a permitted calculation outcome; it does not certify a correct setup. If the curve still looks wrong, inspect the query window, actual samples, labels, and scrape state. Treating every anomaly as normal because extrapolation exists would be as careless as treating every fraction as an incident.

Reset handling needs the individual series

The documentation describes increase as readable shorthand for rate multiplied by the window duration in seconds. It recommends rate for recording rules so increases are tracked consistently per second. That explains the operation’s intended use; it does not require every panel to display the same unit. A window total and a per-second rate answer different reader questions.

The same reference’s rate section says to calculate the rate before aggregating so resets remain detectable for each target. Adding instance counters into one total curve first does not preserve the same reset information. A corresponding window-increase design should likewise retain per-series reset handling instead of shortening the expression at the expense of its meaning.

During a hypothetical rolling release, one instance might restart while another continues growing. Aggregation can conceal the first instance’s reset. This explains why operation order deserves inspection; no numerical comparison between specific expressions was run here. Also verify the metric’s type and business meaning. A name ending in total is not sufficient reason to treat an arbitrary metric as a counter.

Rounding cannot turn an estimate into a ledger

Choose displayed decimal places for the audience and identify the value as a window estimate. Rounding changes presentation. It does not reconstruct uncollected event details or automatically remove window-boundary uncertainty. Fractions may stand out particularly in low-volume services, making an explanation of the measurement more useful than cosmetically replacing them with integers.

If the requirement is exact reconciliation of orders, billing, or individual tasks, use business records suited to that requirement. Monitoring estimates can reveal trends and trigger investigation. Whether a number meets a settlement rule needs evidence from the appropriate records and business definitions.

The article on SLOs and task completion, published on October 8, 2026, discusses measurement denominators and business outcomes. No monitoring instance was queried for this article. Before handing over a panel, ensure maintainers can explain its window, reset handling, and displayed precision, then decide which conclusions the chart can support.
