Why Research with Familiar Users Misses Important Journeys

Familiar users can explain familiar paths, but not always why newcomers never enter them. Match research questions, task conditions, and recruitment channels, then bound conclusions to the evidence actually covered.

M Midnight Engineering notes on development, quality, security, delivery, and operations. View Author Profile
Updated 2026-11-26 · Estimated read 24 min

Article

Recruiting familiar users who respond quickly is useful for checking paths they already know. But if the team needs to understand why first-time users cannot find an entry point, that recruitment can remove crucial evidence before research begins. Sample fit depends not only on whether participants are “real users,” but also on whether their experience, task conditions, and route into recruitment match the decision this round must support. The application journey below is hypothetical: no participants were recruited, interviewed, or tested, and no population rate is estimated.

A recruitment channel determines who can be seen

An active user group, an in-product invitation, and a list of long-standing customers can all find participants quickly. Each has its own entry conditions. A person who sees an in-product invitation has already entered the product. Someone who volunteers in a community may know the terminology and feel comfortable giving feedback. Referrals from support staff may be concentrated among people who previously asked for help. A channel is not a neutral address book; it changes which experiences become part of the research record.

That does not make familiar users unsuitable. Experienced operators can explain frequent tasks, shortcuts, and complex operational constraints. They are especially useful when the decision is whether a release disrupted an established workflow. The problem appears when the conclusion outruns the sample: moving from “these experienced employees completed the task” to “everyone who needs to apply can complete it” requires evidence about newcomers, non-completers, and people who never entered the product.

The GOV.UK participant-recruitment guide recommends recruiting actual or likely users, considering disabled people and people with limited digital skills, and choosing groups, screening criteria, and channels in light of the research question. It also warns against overusing the same participants until they become familiar with research, and notes that timing, location, and recruitment approach can create bias. This is guidance for government service research and a useful methodological reference; it is not a quota standard every organization must copy. Find user research participants

Translate the research question into task conditions

Before recruiting, write the decision the round must support. For example: “Can a first-time applicant find the eligibility explanation and decide what to do next without asking for help?” Then list conditions that could change the answer instead of beginning with a long list of demographic labels. First-time and repeat applicants may differ in whether they remember the entry point. Independent and assisted work can change where help enters the journey. A phone-only context, screen-reader use, or a shared device may change accessibility and privacy concerns. Each condition should answer two questions: why does it matter to this decision, and what will the research observe? If a personal detail will not change the study or its interpretation, collecting it just to complete a form adds little.

The table below is a hypothetical grant-application journey. The question is whether first-time applicants can find the eligibility information, not what share of all applicants succeeds.

Its purpose is to expose breaks between the question, conditions, and routes. GOV.UK’s planning guide similarly asks teams to connect research questions, service phase, and user groups, choosing broad or specific coverage according to what needs to be answered. Screening can quietly turn the study into one about successful users

A common screener question is “Have you successfully submitted an application online?” If only “yes” qualifies, the study cannot hear from people who never found the entry point, abandoned halfway, or switched to an offline route. Branching by experience is more informative: completed; tried but did not complete; or has not tried but may need the service. These branches do not all require the same method. An interview can explore how someone understands the process; observing a page task requires appropriate task materials and instructions. The important point is not to disguise “can smoothly demonstrate this product” as an irrelevant sign-up requirement.

Review the recruitment brief and screener together. GOV.UK’s brief-writing guide recommends including session dates and times, duration, location, participant count, participant types, and criteria. It explicitly cautions that recruiters may exclude disabled people and people with limited digital skills by default unless the brief welcomes them. It also advises checking that the screener does not filter out intended participants. Write a recruitment brief

A study team can record screener outcomes as eligible, not currently eligible, unanswered, withdrawn, or not reached, with a short reason for exclusion. This does not require publishing sensitive personal information or giving every observer access to a complete contact list. What matters is a coverage audit: which experiences may be missing, whether the criteria or channel created that gap, and which claim is limited as a result. Refusal or non-attendance does not reveal the experience of people who declined; it simply prevents the team from treating attendees as the whole audience.

Participation arrangements can become another filter

Timing, location, devices, and compensation influence who can take part. A weekday daytime session may exclude shift workers. Requiring a research-team device may be unsuitable for someone who needs a particular assistive-technology setup. A long screener can cause someone who is already tired or less confident online to leave. An invitation that says “everyone is welcome” is not enough: the brief should describe negotiable ways to participate, and the confirmation process should ask what support is needed.

Data collection should also be restrained. To arrange a session, the team may need to know that someone needs more time, a remote session, or compatibility with particular assistive technology. It may not need a diagnosis, identity document, or detailed history unrelated to the question. Before the study, explain what will be recorded, who can see it, how long it will be kept, and how participation can be withdrawn. Access should match the actual analytic need. A recruitment agency finding participants does not transfer these responsibilities away from the team.

Participant numbers also need a method-specific explanation. The GOV.UK guide gives 4–8 people as a typical number to recruit for one interview or usability-testing round, while saying that the number depends on the method. This is neither a universal law nor a basis for estimating population proportions from qualitative research. Several rounds for different questions, tasks, or specialist roles may be clearer than placing everyone in one round. Conversely, if the question concerns one narrow expert operation, broad recruitment may add little relevant evidence. The team needs to explain why the sample can answer the question, not use a single number as proof of adequate coverage. For a service with a broad audience, the planning guide recommends including disabled users and people who may need support in every round. Smaller or specialist groups may instead need dedicated rounds to examine significant barriers and support arrangements. That qualification shows why broad inclusion does not mean putting every group into every study question.

When familiar users are exactly the right participants

Suppose a team wants to learn whether a new release changed the batch operation used daily by senior reviewers. People who currently perform that review work are appropriate participants; newcomers cannot replace their knowledge of exceptions and operational rhythm. The conclusion should say what changed in the observed expert task, while onboarding or first-use questions are tested separately. A narrow sample is not a defect when the question is narrow. It becomes a problem only when the team generalizes the result to roles the study did not include.

Accordingly, neither fairness nor research validity is achieved by mechanically inviting one person from every category. Some questions require a particular qualification or level of experience; other questions require a route into research for people who have not completed the task. The deciding factor is the task and decision, not a blanket label that familiar users are biased or demographic quotas that merely look representative.

How to check whether this round can answer its question

Before sending invitations, ask the research lead five things: What decision is this round supporting? Which experiences or conditions could change the task outcome? Who can each recruitment route reach, and whom can it not reach? Which people could the screener exclude? If a key condition has no participants, how will the conclusion be narrowed or the next round supplement the evidence?

Then compare the brief with the actual recruitment record. If the invitation welcomes assistive-technology users but the screener and session setup cannot support the relevant devices, the problem is not a small copy edit; the promise conflicts with the participation conditions.

Reports should keep three layers separate: behavior directly observed, the team’s explanation for it, and experiences still missing. In the hypothetical example, “a participant did not find the eligibility information” is an observation; “the label was unfamiliar” is a hypothesis to test; and “people who never reached the online service did not participate” is a coverage limitation. The next round might change the channel or task material to test the explanation, but the three layers should not become the sweeping statement “users generally do not understand it.”

For page-task evaluation, see neutral usability task design, while keeping recruitment fit separate from moderation neutrality: the first determines who can provide evidence, the second can influence how a participant performs. For the boundary between research evidence and interpretation, see separating observation from interpretation and consent records and data lineage. These are related site articles; they do not mean the recruitment example here was executed.

The method proposed here is auditable, but no recruitment or research was conducted. In review, do not ask only whether “users” appear on the participant list. Check whether the research question, task conditions, channels, and screening records connect. If a condition central to the decision is missing, narrow the report or conduct a suitable follow-up round before extending the finding to a wider audience.
