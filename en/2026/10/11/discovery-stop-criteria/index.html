When Is Discovery Finished? When the Next Decision Is Defensible

Discovery should not end because a quota of interviews or prototypes was met. Put problem evidence, alternatives, hard constraints, and decisive unknowns on one decision map before choosing to proceed, stop, or narrow the next test.

M Midnight Engineering notes on development, quality, security, delivery, and operations. View Author Profile
Updated 2026-11-26 · Estimated read 28 min
Translations
zh-CN en

Article

Discovery is finished when a team can explain, with bounded evidence, whether to proceed, stop, or narrow the next test—not when it reaches a quota of interviews, competitor reviews, or prototypes. “Finished” means that the team can make a phase decision and state its limits. Uncertainty will remain; ending discovery does not erase it. Teams often mistake discovery for an admission ticket to development: once the research activities are complete, the project is expected to receive engineering capacity. That turns discovery into packaging for a solution that was already chosen.

Separate a phase decision from certainty

Discovery is not a guarantee of future success, nor is it an exercise in eliminating every risk. It separates several questions: do users face a problem worth solving; is the difficulty caused by their current approach important enough; is there a feasible path to improve it; must that path be a new digital service; and which unknowns could change the investment decision? Answering only the first question may establish that a problem exists while leaving no evidence that a particular feature will solve it.

The GOV.UK discovery guide says teams should understand users’ goals, context, constraints, and opportunities for improvement before building a service. The decision about alpha follows from this work; service construction should not start while discovery continues. That same page says stopping is not a failure when research supports that choice. Its phase names, spending controls, and organizational arrangements are specific to UK government and should not be copied as enterprise rules. The transferable idea here is the decision principle: understand the problem before choosing the next step. Its duration section gives around four to eight weeks as typical, but says there is no fixed duration and the purpose should determine the time spent. That is not a quota for every project.

A useful way to prevent a hidden promise is to write the phase exit as a sentence: “What do we know, which path does that support, and what remains unknown—and why does that unknown not block this choice?” If a report says only “proceed to development” but does not explain why content governance, process changes, or an existing third-party service are inadequate alternatives, the team has not compared options. Conversely, explaining why a new service may be needed and naming the one assumption the next phase must test does not mean that every design detail has been approved.

Test the decision map with a hypothetical case

The following case is illustrative, not a real customer study. A team receives a request to “add intelligent search.” If it defines the problem as “users need AI search,” discovery becomes a choice among models, search boxes, and ranking methods. A more useful problem statement might be: “How can employees confirm that an operating document applies to the current version and whether it is still valid?” That opens several paths: search may help, but so might consistent naming, an accountable owner, visible revision dates, or an easier route to the existing repository.

List decisions before listing features: stop the current service proposal; repair document governance first; run a small prototype to see whether version information changes a user's choice; or move to alpha after permissions and data provenance are confirmed. Each option should be vulnerable to evidence. If document owners cannot say which files are current, a ranking prototype cannot answer whether the content is valid. If permissions do not allow documents to be consolidated for indexing, an attractive search prototype does not justify a production service.

A compact evidence ledger is enough; it need not become a weighted scoring model:

Judgment Supporting evidence now Counterevidence or gap Next step that could change the decision
Users struggle to tell whether a document applies Observed task records or user statements (none supplied here) It may be occasional, a training gap, or stale content Inspect real task samples and separate retrieval failure from invalid content
A new service is better than process repair No evidence yet Ownership, naming, or review dates may be the cause Ask content owners to establish the status of a real, bounded document set
Data can be used lawfully and safely Not confirmed Permissions, contracts, and retention rules may preclude aggregation Ask accountable data and compliance owners to confirm a permitted scope
A prototype can test the main risk Depends on what it displays Smooth clicks do not establish content correctness Have participants complete a version-selection task and record error types

“None supplied” matters. It keeps a hypothesis from being rewritten as a research finding. Real samples are necessary before the team can say the problem is widespread; a cost-saving percentage cannot be invented. The purpose of the next test is to distinguish explanations. If users can locate a file but cannot identify its valid version, improving ranking may not be the first thing to test. If assigning an owner and a revision date changes the difficulty, the root problem may be governance rather than retrieval.

A hypothetical pattern (not interview notes) makes the branches visible: one person cannot find a file; another finds it but cannot tell which version applies; a third knows which file to use but finds no accountable owner. The first observation may point to navigation or retrieval, the second to version information, and the third to governance. If all three are collapsed into “search is poor,” a prototype that helps the first person find a file faster has not answered the other two problems. When comparing options, record why people chose as they did on the same task and material, and retain “could not determine” as a failure type instead of flattening it into one satisfaction judgment. This is a hypothetical illustration of discrimination, not a sample proportion or measured result.

For every unknown, record its direction of impact, who can answer it, and when an answer is available. An unknown deserves early investigation only if it might change the decision. Whether users like a chat-style interface may not affect whether document validity needs improvement; whether the source material can be handled within permission boundaries may determine the service path. Unknowns can be grouped as those that affect whether the problem is worth solving, those that affect the choice of solution, and those that affect later implementation details. For that reason, work in discovery should prioritize the first two. The third usually belongs in a later, targeted check rather than an indefinite extension of research.\n\nA phase decision should also name counterevidence: what observable signal supports the current path, what result would stop or redirect it, who will gather the evidence, and when the decision will be revisited. If the provisional choice is to repair document governance first, state what would overturn it: after owners and valid dates are clear, the target task still often cannot locate the applicable file, so navigation or retrieval needs another look. The condition must be observable and specify which tasks and participants it covers; this article did not run that check. A decision with no countercondition can turn “proceed” into an unchallengeable commitment at the next review.

Proceed, stop, narrow, and defer are different outcomes

Proceeding to the next phase requires at least one valuable and feasible direction, a named assumption, and a plan for how to disprove it. “Entering alpha” should not be described as approval to build a finished service. Under the GOV.UK phase description, alpha is a decision to investigate the problem further. It says discovery should leave ideas to test, an order for testing them, a rough view of the needed team, and a way to measure success. A general product team might adapt these into a narrow prototype or technical experiment, but that adaptation is an editorial practice judgment, not an official mandatory template.

Stopping means that the current proposal does not merit further investment, or the likely value does not justify the cost. Stopping a proposal does not deny that the user problem existed. If the problem is real but its cause is missing document ownership, stopping an intelligent-search proposal and repairing governance may be the more effective decision. To make a stop reusable, preserve the evidence, what remains unproved, and conditions for reopening—for example, if substantial version-selection failures remain after an ownership process is established, or a new compliance path makes previously unavailable data usable. If reopening conditions are missing, another team may begin again from the same assumptions.

Narrowing is appropriate when a direction may work but the largest uncertainty remains unresolved. Do not use one large prototype to answer ten questions. Choose the smallest discriminating test: for example, ask a small number of participants to use the same documents with “search results only” and with “results plus revision owner and date,” then observe whether they can identify the applicable file. This is a proposed test design, not an executed experiment. Before running it, decide what behavior will be observed, which counterexample would change the decision, and what conclusion the sample can support. A participant completing a task shows only that this person completed this task with this material and under these conditions. That result says nothing by itself about universal usability or production accuracy.

Deferring is different: a decisive hard constraint remains unresolved, but an accountable owner and an answer date exist. Law or contract boundaries may not be removable by changing the interface. A legacy-system restriction may be hard, or it may become a soft constraint through a process change. The guide’s constraint section recommends distinguishing difficult-to-change constraints from existing processes that can be changed. Recording both as “technical limitations” can lead a team to work around a rule while overlooking alternatives. A deferral needs the evidence for the constraint, the decision owner, a review date, and an exit route if the answer is still no. Otherwise it is merely a renamed backlog item.

Two efficient-looking habits create false closure

The first is using activity counts as completion criteria. “Interview ten people, build three prototypes, review five competitors” is checkable but does not guarantee that the important unknown has been answered. If the tenth interview still cannot distinguish stale content from failed retrieval, meeting the quota has not made a solution decision possible. Conversely, if existing data and a small amount of research have already ruled out the major options, forcing more activity wastes time. The published guidance says there is no fixed discovery duration and that its purpose should determine the time spent. A calendar and activity list can help manage work; neither substitutes for a decision condition.

The second is translating every risk into “do more research.” That gives exploration no exit. Ask first whether the risk changes the phase decision, then whether a low-cost test could distinguish the plausible explanations. Some risks can only be observed at real scale, and a small prototype may say little about them. In that case, state that the risk will enter a later phase with limited scope, rollback, and monitoring rather than pretending discovery can eliminate it. The opposite case matters too: if an unverified authorization boundary could expose data to the wrong users, prototype usability is no reason to carry the risk forward. The accountable authorization owner needs to resolve that boundary.

Hand over the evidence boundary, not a slogan

At exit, the next team should be able to see the user's concrete task; the current approach and its failure; evidence for and against the proposed path; alternatives; hard constraints and their owners; the unknown most likely to change the decision; the reason to proceed, stop, narrow, or defer; and the first validation plus its failure condition. The handoff should also state where the evidence came from, when it was gathered, whom it covered, and which groups or contexts were not observed. Accessibility, digital skills, connectivity, and offline steps can change the problem boundary. It also includes user context, accessibility needs, and digital skills in discovery. This does not require product teams to copy a UK government form. It guards against studying only the easiest users to reach and turning a local observation into a universal claim.

Recent-task interviews can help a team understand user tasks, but an interview count does not prove the conclusion. Turning material into decision evidence requires stating how participants relate to the intended population, how questions were asked, what was observed, and what other explanations remain. If the material is still insufficient, label the gap as unknown and state whether it blocks the current choice. A visible unknown is more useful than unjustified certainty.

No actual discovery project, user interview, prototype, or cost data was supplied for this article. The document-search scenario, evidence ledger, and experiment are hypothetical examples used to explain a decision method, not an execution report. In a review meeting, ask: which evidence supports proceeding; which could overturn it; what are the stop and alternative paths; and what failure condition applies to the next test? If the team cannot answer those questions, the missing deliverable is decision evidence—not another polished phase presentation.
