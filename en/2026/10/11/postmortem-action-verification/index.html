How can you verify an incident action without overstating risk reduction?

Turn postmortem actions into falsifiable acceptance checks, distinguishing a closed task, a tested scenario, and a longer-term change in risk.

M Midnight Engineering notes on development, quality, security, delivery, and operations. View Author Profile
Updated 2026-11-26 · Estimated read 27 min
Translations
zh-CN en

Article

A task marked done proves that the requested work was delivered; it does not by itself prove that the failure path changed. A scenario passes only against a predeclared input, system response, and counterexample that could fail the check. A claim of lower risk needs suitable operational observation and exposure data. Keep the conclusion at the level the evidence supports.

Write an action as a hypothesis

Moving a task from open to done shows that someone delivered the work requested by that task. It does not automatically prove that the system's failure path changed. A test that triggers the expected alert shows that the signal and notification path responded under that set of conditions; it does not by itself show that production problems are now less frequent. When these claims are collapsed, a postmortem can have a polished closure list and no system evidence that another engineer can inspect.

Google's SRE postmortem chapter lists preventing recurrence or reducing impact among the goals of postmortems. The organization's public action-item examples also include an owner, tracking number, priority, and verifiable end state; the SRE Workbook's postmortem practices says factual conclusions should link to supporting data. These are public descriptions of that organization's practice, not a requirement that every team copy a specific process. The practical judgment here is narrower: treat each corrective action as a falsifiable hypothesis about the system. State which failure condition could previously pass through the system and which boundary the change is meant to block, detect, limit, or recover from it.

Imagine that an asynchronous export queue once accumulated work for a long time. An on-call engineer noticed the backlog by chance while looking at a dashboard for a new feature, then scaled capacity and manually recovered the queue. The postmortem says “add monitoring.” If a maintainer adds a chart and closes the task, the code delivery may be complete while the original discovery problem remains. Someone who is not subscribed to the dashboard may never see it. A notification without an owner may not lead to a response. Even if it fires, the on-call guide may still lack recovery steps. This is a hypothetical example, not a report of an actual incident.

Keep delivery status and evidence separate

An action can have three related but distinct states. The first is work status: was the code, configuration, or process change merged, released, or handed off? The second is acceptance status: did the predeclared input condition, observation point, and pass criterion hold in the named environment? The third is an effectiveness judgment: did the original user impact or failure path become less frequent, less severe, or easier to detect in representative operation? The evidence differs. A merge record supports the first statement. A controlled scenario supports the second. The third also depends on an observation window, a denominator, data quality, and concurrent changes.

When an action looks like an ordinary to-do, acceptance can become “it seems finished.” “Add monitoring,” “improve the alert,” and “update the documentation” do not say what result counts as success. A reviewable action can record the failure path, the system condition to change, an observable signal, an acceptance predicate, the allowed environment and method, a counterexample, an owner and due date, dependencies, residual risk, and a recheck trigger. Not every small change needs a long form. A few precise sentences in the issue can be enough. The important point is that another person can understand the acceptance condition before implementation starts and can make it fail in a test.

For the hypothetical export backlog, “add a task-duration chart” is not an acceptance condition. The hypothesis could instead say: “When the oldest unfinished task reaches the product's agreed wait objective, the system emits a notification labeled with the service, queue, and impact scope, routes it to the current responder, and the responder can distinguish a stalled consumer from reduced processing capacity using the runbook.” The wait objective should not be copied from a postmortem template; it should come from user tolerance and the service's existing objective. The evidence should name the test environment, configuration version, input, and notification recipient. A proposed check must not be written as though a successful drill has already happened.

Even this acceptance result does not prove that incident probability fell. At most, it establishes that a detection path worked under the named test conditions and records the coverage boundary. It does not show that every backlog produces the same alert, much less that every responder can restore service after receiving it. “Threshold and routing verified in the test environment” is more defensible than “problem solved.”

Make it possible for the check to fail

Acceptance should include counterexamples in advance. For an alert change, check both sides of the threshold, missing labels, silencing, an empty routing destination, a notification without service context, and whether the recipient can find the next step. If the test only confirms that a rule exists in a configuration file, it mistakes existence for usability. For a process change, simulate an unavailable dependency, an absent key role, or an unconfirmed handoff and check whether the process identifies the next owner rather than merely proving that a paragraph was added to a document.

Negative tests are not a reason to create an unapproved production incident. They can use an isolated test environment, staging, synthetic signals, or a low-risk exercise, while documenting differences from production. If only static configuration can be checked and the full notification path cannot be triggered, limit the conclusion to “configuration checked.” The workbook's examples emphasize verifiable end states and actions aimed at prevention or mitigation; “verifiable” does not mean one test covers every environment.

Also ask whether the acceptance scenario represents the original failure path. Suppose the consumer stopped but the dashboard showed only that the request API was healthy. Checking the API's health endpoint does not cover a stalled consumer. Trace the scenario from trigger to user impact: failure input or state, system behavior, observable signal, notification route, response action, and data consistency after recovery. If a step is replaced by an adjacent but non-equivalent check, record the scope limit. Otherwise the team may read “neighboring component passed” as “the original issue is fixed.”

Different actions require different evidence

Google's incident-management guide also includes detection, mitigation, coordination, and communication among improvement targets. This article groups the acceptance problem into prevention, detection, mitigation, and recovery so evidence can match the intended change; it is not presented as the only valid taxonomy.

Preventive actions should test whether the trigger is constrained or a dangerous state is harder to reach. For example, limiting the resources affected by one operation calls for a test that verifies boundary resources cannot be changed by that operation. A counterexample is a test that covers the normal resource set but omits permission or tenant boundaries; passing the happy path does not establish that every cross-boundary path is blocked.

Detection actions should check the whole path from signal production to responder notification, including whether noise and silences can suppress the target signal. Seeing a time series change does not prove that paging is reachable. A delivery test can show that a message arrived at a channel, but not that the on-call person understood the impact or knew the first mitigation step. Faster detection also does not mean the failure no longer occurs.

Mitigation actions focus on limiting user impact after a failure begins: a circuit breaker, isolation, degraded mode, or traffic shift. Acceptance should check when the protection activates, which functions remain usable, which requests are rejected, and whether recovery needs compensation. Verifying that a switch can be turned on is not enough if a partial dependency failure could instead cause a site-wide rejection.

Recovery actions concern the steps, data, and permissions needed to return to an acceptable service state. An updated guide is not a verified recovery. A bounded check could ask whether the backup is readable, the role can obtain required permissions, and the data reconciliation condition is explicit. Any real recovery exercise needs approval, a defined isolation scope, and a rollback plan. This article does not recommend unapproved production fault injection.

Passing a scenario is not observing lower risk

To describe a change in risk, first define the event. “Queue wait exceeded the user objective” and “a service-level incident occurred” are different events. Counting only incident tickets misses near misses without user impact; counting alerts may turn noise into failures. The observation window should match exposure: export volume, queue workload, or time in service could be a relevant denominator, depending on the question. Also record traffic changes, releases, seasonality, and dependency updates that may affect the result.

Even if a similar event does not recur in a short post-change window, the system may simply have had fewer opportunities to encounter it. The action can report, “No instance of the defined event was observed during this interval,” without converting that statement into a percentage reduction in risk. When events are rare, before-and-after rates are highly uncertain. It may be better to observe longer, record exposure, or leave the result explicitly unknown than to manufacture precision. The workbook also encourages linking conclusions to source data. The denominator and confounder analysis here is editorial advice, not a verbatim description of that organization's policy.

If a similar failure returns, do not immediately label the action ineffective. It may have targeted only one trigger, improved detection without changing prevention, or become invalid after system conditions changed. Revisit the original hypothesis, the new runtime evidence, uncovered boundaries, and related actions before expanding the check or reopening the task. Conversely, a long period without recurrence does not prove effectiveness if the system has not encountered the relevant input.

When to close, and when to leave the result unknown

Record task closure and the effectiveness conclusion separately. For example: “Rule deployed; a synthetic backlog signal in staging triggered the expected route; the test did not cover high-concurrency grouping under production traffic; whether discovery time improved in production remains under observation.” That allows the delivery work to be complete without broadening the evidence. If an action only requires completing documentation, the acceptance condition might be that a responder can use the guide to find the recovery steps in a named scenario, not that the page count increased.

Before closing, check four things: was the acceptance condition defined before the work; does the result identify environment, configuration, and input; is there at least one counterexample that could make the check fail; and is uncovered residual risk accepted, scheduled, or assigned a review date? Organizations can use different follow-up mechanisms. In a Google SRE Prodcast discussion, participants say teams may use different follow-up mechanisms, but follow-up must be ensured; the coordinator need not personally implement every change. Ownership means coordination and follow-through, not assigning blame for an incident.

When permissions, traffic paths, alert routing, dependency versions, or product objectives change, the original acceptance evidence may no longer apply. Reopen the action or create an explicit replacement check if it depended on a metric that is being removed or a paging route that is obsolete. If similar failures happen repeatedly, examine whether actions are deep enough, whether reliability work keeps losing priority to feature work, or whether isolated patches share a systemic cause. Repeatedly adding small fixes is not a substitute for that review.

A postmortem action is not just a to-do list. It is a traceable hypothesis about how the system failed, what condition should change, how to test it, and what evidence remains missing. Write down one failure path that previously passed through the system; classify the intervention as prevention, detection, mitigation, or recovery; then make the acceptance statement falsifiable. The team can then say “code delivered,” “this scenario passed,” or “the risk change remains unknown” without collapsing them into a premature green status.

The export, alert, and acceptance examples in this article are design proposals. No real incident materials were reviewed, no drill was run, and no risk change was measured.
