How should you interpret a survey rating that combines speed and quality?

A single rating for response speed and resolution quality hides which one needs attention. Learn how to split the question, test its meaning, retain a useful overall measure and handle trend breaks.

M Midnight Engineering notes on development, quality, security, delivery, and operations. View Author Profile
Updated 2026-11-24 · Estimated read 22 min
Translations
zh-CN en

Article

Key takeaways

  • One combined score cannot identify the separate contribution of speed and resolution quality.
  • An item needs an event, recall window, eligibility path and decision use.
  • Changing the question creates a trend-comparison break; testing and bridging are proposed, not performed here.

When a survey asks respondents to rate speed and quality in one question, its single score cannot tell a team which one to improve. Separate the concepts that drive different decisions, and give people who have no relevant experience, cannot remember it or have an unresolved case an appropriate path. Keep an overall question only when it measures a deliberately defined overall judgment. The purpose of splitting a question is not to inflate the survey; it is to make the answer actionable.

One score can represent opposing experiences

Consider the question, “How satisfied are you with the speed and quality of customer support?” on a five-point scale. One person may receive a quick reply that does not solve the problem. Another may wait a long time but eventually receive an accurate resolution. Both could choose 3, yet their experiences call for different changes. A third person may have received only an initial response and still be waiting for a resolution. That respondent is not necessarily rating the second part poorly; they may not be able to answer it yet.

Once combined, the analyst sees a number but cannot tell whether it is a compromise between two judgments, one dimension dominating the other, or a respondent interpreting the prompt as an overall rating of support. A larger sample can estimate the mixed distribution more precisely, but it cannot mathematically recover dimensions the question never captured. Correlating the total score with ticket volume or response time can offer clues, but it does not prove whether speed or resolution quality drove a rating unless another design identifies the cause.

Pew Research Center’s survey-question guidance describes questions that ask respondents to evaluate multiple concepts as difficult to answer and interpret, and emphasizes that wording affects how people understand a question. AAPOR’s best practices for survey research recommend focusing each question on one concept, using specific language, and making response options non-overlapping and reasonably complete. These are methodological recommendations, not an experiment conducted for this article or evidence that every question containing multiple words is invalid. The relevant test is whether respondents must make one response for multiple objects that can vary independently.

Begin with the decision the score should change

Before splitting a question, write down who will use the result and what action they may take. A staffing decision may call for knowing whether first human response arrives within an agreed peak-period window. For a help-content redesign, the more relevant outcome may be whether users can complete their task. Those decisions imply different measures. An existing satisfaction dashboard is not, by itself, a research objective.

One possible split is: “For your most recent completed support request, how would you rate the time until the first human response?” and “Was the problem that led you to contact support resolved?” The first prompt defines an event and a time boundary. The second can first ask whether the request is complete and then ask about its outcome. Response options should match the audience and meaning. They might include “not yet complete,” “resolved,” “partly resolved,” “not resolved,” “do not remember” or “not applicable.” Those are examples to evaluate, not universal answer choices. If “partly resolved” does not guide an action, or a request can have several goals, the researcher needs to define the outcome more carefully. People should be able to skip or decline to judge when they cannot reasonably answer.

The example avoids leaving “speed” as an undefined idea. An automatic acknowledgment, first human reply, useful next step and final closure are different events. Likewise, “quality” could mean accuracy, completeness, courtesy, task completion or avoiding a repeat contact. A survey must identify the event to recall, the time window and the object being rated. Deleting the word “and” does not make answers comparable if people are still recalling different events.

Not every multi-dimensional idea must become several questions. If the research objective is explicitly to ask “How was the overall experience?”, a clearly labeled overall question can serve that purpose. It measures an overall judgment, not a diagnostic score for speed and quality. It is insufficient when a team needs to change the two dimensions separately. A validated scale may use multiple items to measure one pre-defined theoretical construct; that differs from an improvised prompt that combines two service dimensions in one rating. A conjoint or trade-off task may deliberately ask respondents to compare attributes, but it should make the comparison explicit instead of compressing two evaluations into a satisfaction star.

Test interpretation, not just the wording

Splitting one prompt into two is a text edit. A more useful pretest asks target respondents to explain what they think each question means, which event they recall, how they interpret “response” and “resolution,” whether they can identify the time point, and why a particular option fits. The researcher can then compare how different people interpret the same terms instead of merely asking whether the question “is clear.” The UK Government Analysis Function’s questionnaire-design guidance defines a double-barrelled question as two questions in one, recommends splitting it, and gives separate ratings for the course facilitator and course materials as an example. It also identifies cognitive testing as a way to check understanding and questionnaire quality.

These interviews or cognitive pretests are recommended validation steps; they were not conducted with users or a real team for this article. A small set of target readers can reveal wording problems, but the appropriate sample depends on the research purpose. A few successful paraphrases do not prove that all groups will interpret a question in the same way. For audiences across languages or job roles, check terminology, reading context and whether people have any relevant experience. Translation also needs to preserve the event being measured, not just match words.

Look for three kinds of failure. First, do some people understand “response” as the first reply while others mean final resolution? Second, does one response option mean both “nothing happened” and “it happened but went badly”? Third, does the routing send an open case to a question intended only for completed cases? If answers trigger follow-up questions, the interaction flow is part of the measurement design. Respondents encounter explanations, answer options and prior questions as context. AAPOR notes that earlier questions can affect later answers; changing the order of response choices does not guarantee the context has no effect. The UK guidance page lists a publication date of 14 March 2023 and currently says it is under review; this article treats it as a traceable design reference, not a binding cross-industry standard.

After a pretest, revise the event definition, answer options and routing together. For example, if “not yet complete” routes to a status question, a respondent should not be forced to guess about resolution. If a first response has not arrived, a response-time rating may need a separate “no response yet” branch or a defined cutoff. That branch should not be confused with an extremely slow response; one is a censored or incomplete observation, while the other is an observed duration. Whether to measure duration directly or ask for a subjective rating depends on the decision and the data available.

A split question changes the meaning of historical data

Suppose an old questionnaire asks, “Are you satisfied with speed and quality?” and the new one separately asks about response wait and resolution. A 4 on the old measure and a 4 on the new measure are not automatically the same result. Even if service did not change, clearer wording might lead respondents to answer only from one dimension. If the respondent source, survey channel, recall window or answer options also changed, several parts of the measurement process may explain the difference.

If a time series matters, retain the old wording for a limited transition or test the old and new versions in parallel, or use another bridge design chosen in advance. The right approach depends on whether the same people can receive both questions, whether memory effects are likely, and whether the sampling frame and mode remain stable. Pew’s guidance documents that wording and context can influence responses. AAPOR recommends keeping wording, framing and methods similar for comparisons over time; when a change is necessary, it discusses split-ballot designs as one way to test sensitivity. None of this proves that a bridge will fully identify a causal change. The team should record the measure change and describe the limits of attribution.

Keep the exact wording, answer options, routing conditions, target population, survey mode and effective date with the analysis. If old responses cannot be decomposed, label the break rather than estimating how much of the old score came from response speed using a separate ticket metric. The original wording is part of the evidence needed to interpret a dataset, not disposable presentation copy.

When should an overall question remain?

Ask three questions. First, if a response is low, can the team name an action to take? Second, could a respondent rate the component experiences differently? Third, does an overall result have a separate use? If the first two indicate a need to distinguish dimensions and the third has no clear purpose, split or remove the extra question. If an overall measure such as recommendation intent or overall experience supports a defined decision, keep it as its own construct. Do not substitute an average of speed and quality ratings for it unless that aggregation has a justified interpretation.

Before launch, verify how each response enters the data: are missing, skipped, not-yet-complete, no-contact and “do not know” states distinct? Do answer categories overlap? Can a person change an answer? Can a report display response speed separately from resolution status? These are proposed checks, not a claim that a system acceptance test was run here. As with defining the purpose of each form question, every field should trace to a use. If a response will not change a decision or test an assumption, reconsider whether it is worth the burden on respondents.

The final test is not whether the question sounds more sophisticated. It is whether a team can distinguish the actions implied by the answers and state who could not answer and which results cannot be compared. If the split questions still leave the event, recall period or decision unclear, redefine the measurement problem instead of polishing the sentence.
