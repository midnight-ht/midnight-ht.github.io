Choose Automation Work by First Identifying Toil

Compare removing demand, durable engineering and automation while accounting for routine work, review, maintenance and recovery.

M Midnight Engineering notes on development, quality, security, delivery, and operations. View Author Profile
Updated 2026-11-24 · Estimated read 16 min

Article

Key takeaways

  • Toil is operational burden, not personal dislike.
  • Confirm demand before comparing removal, redesign and automation.
  • Assumption-based arithmetic is not measured team savings.

Not necessarily. First ask whether the work is tied to operating a production service, repeated, and mostly tactical human handling; then compare removing the demand, making a durable engineering change, and automating the remaining work. Automation is one possible response to toil, not the default response. If nobody uses the result anymore, stopping the process may be more effective than scripting it.

Toil is not just work someone dislikes

The Google SRE Book chapter, “Eliminating Toil” describes toil as work associated with running a production service that tends to be manual, repetitive, automatable, tactical, lacking enduring value, and linear with service growth. The chapter says a task need not have every characteristic to be a candidate. The issue is its relationship to operating the service, not whether an individual finds it boring.

A manual click may be tedious but necessary during a first investigation of a novel failure. Conversely, manually starting a script still requires operator attention; having a script does not mean toil has disappeared. The chapter distinguishes administrative overhead from production toil and treats alert-configuration cleanup as engineering when it leaves a lasting improvement, even if the cleanup is mundane. This article uses that established framework; it does not turn Google's organizational goal into a quota for every team.

Ask whether the result is still needed

Write the work as a traceable chain: who triggers it and when, where the input comes from, who uses the output to decide, and what happens if the work is skipped or wrong. This often changes what the “automation script” is supposed to do. If a routine report has no readers, stopping its generation and maintenance may be enough. If it is required evidence for approval, deleting it hides a risk. If an operator must judge whether an action could harm production, that judgment may be part of the responsibility; separate repetitive data gathering from final authorization.

Also ask why the task recurs. Is copying a result between two systems an intentional audit boundary or a missing interface? A one-time API, alert-routing, or data-model change may take more engineering time while preventing future scale from creating proportional manual work. Google's definition also cautions against declaring a task “not toil” merely because human judgment appears necessary. A poor alert or complex design may have created unnecessary judgment work, though response work still exists until the redesign is implemented.

Compare three options with stated assumptions

The following numbers are arithmetic assumptions, not measurements. Suppose a team runs the same status query 12 times a week, with six minutes of operator time per run for starting it, checking, and copying its output. The baseline is 72 operator minutes per week. Option A removes an unused report; if there are truly no downstream consumers, the 72 minutes and the script itself can disappear. Option B adds a stable interface between the systems and is assumed to take 16 engineering hours up front. In this scenario, all 72 minutes come from query and copy work, with no separate necessary approval, so the interface could remove that repetition. If an approval carries business or safety responsibility, list it separately; it is not time the design may simply automate away. Option C automates the work, but retains one minute of human review per run, 18 minutes of weekly maintenance, and a 15-minute weekly allowance for recovery. Its assumed weekly burden is 12+18+15=45 minutes, nominally 27 minutes below baseline.

The assumptions also expose the limits of payback arithmetic. If automation takes eight hours to build, dividing by 27 minutes saved per week gives about 17.8 weeks to offset that initial effort. If the interface change costs 16 hours and removes all 72 weekly minutes, the same simple calculation is about 13.3 weeks. Neither calculation prices incorrect outcomes, rollout review, workload changes, handoff to another maintainer, or risk reduction; both assume future frequency is stable. More fundamentally, Option A may not need a payback calculation at all: if the need is confirmed to have disappeared, continuing to provide an unused result has no value.

The numbers reveal assumptions; they do not rank the options. If human review is a non-negotiable safety approval, automation should cover preparation and collection, not silently remove authorization. If the query supports incident recovery rather than a routine report, removing it could create an unacceptable blind spot; an interface change also needs a fallback for interface failure. Automation may not eliminate work. It may transfer time from routine operators to script maintainers or people handling failures.

Failure paths determine whether a script reduces toil

A script can work while inputs stay stable and fail repeatedly after a schema changes. An operator may still need to inspect the alert, decide whether retry is safe, repair permissions or field mappings, inspect side effects, and recover manually. For an irreversible action, automatic retries may create duplicate changes. Counting only the seconds for a normal run misses the riskiest path. At minimum, distinguish routine review, dependency updates, permission rotation, partial success, duplicate requests, and recovery.

Scope matters too. For reads, automatically collect data while leaving interpretation to a human. For writes, first generate a recommendation or preview for approval. Broader autonomous execution is easier to justify for stable, low-impact, idempotent actions. An AI agent handling unstructured input also needs allowed data sources, tool permissions, a verifiable outcome, and explicit refusal or handoff conditions. Fluent model text or a successful tool response does not prove production state changed. Verify the result against an authoritative state source rather than treating plausible language as acceptance evidence.

What evidence supports continuing, redesigning, or stopping?

Observe for a defined period and record each trigger, human minutes, success and failure, recovery actions, result consumers, error consequences, and maintenance effort. Then ask whether the demand exists, whether a one-time design change can remove repeated steps, what judgment and recovery remain after automation, and how workload changes with scale. A short observation window may miss rare failures; list unobserved risks rather than interpreting zero observed failures as zero risk.

Acceptance should include counterexamples: how the system stops when input fields change; whether permission loss fails safely; how partial completion is identified; whether repeated requests are idempotent; and whether a human taking over can find an operation record. These are proposed checks for a reader's team. This article did not run an evaluation and claims no saved minutes or reliability improvement. The Google chapter reports that its SRE organization used an average toil-below-50% target and describes that target in its own organizational context; it is not a standard all teams should copy. The official Google SRE site advertises a completely rewritten second edition and says practice evolved over the decade, so the older chapter is a useful definition framework, not a complete statement of current detailed policy.

Verifying postmortem actions marks a related boundary: filing an improvement task does not establish that operational work fell. Record demand, enduring engineering, routine operations, and failure recovery separately. When evidence is thin, observe longer or keep automation narrow rather than claiming time savings.
