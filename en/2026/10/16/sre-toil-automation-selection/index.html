Choose automation work by toil characteristics

Assess toil characteristics and failure workload before selecting automation investments.

M Midnight Engineering notes on development, quality, security, delivery, and operations. View Author Profile
Updated 2026-10-16 · Estimated read 7 min
Translations
zh-CN en

Article

Repeated work does not automatically deserve automation priority. First assess toil characteristics: manual, repetitive, automatable, tactical, lacking enduring value and scaling linearly with service growth. Compare automating a task with removing the underlying need for it.

Tedious work is not the only criterion

The Google SRE Book chapter Eliminating Toil defines these characteristics without requiring every task to meet all of them. It distinguishes administrative overhead and tedious engineering work that creates lasting value, such as cleaning up alert configuration. This is an established historical framework, not newly announced policy or evidence of a measured industry return.

Imagine repeatedly running a status query and copying results into another system. Before automating clicks, establish why the work exists. Missing integration may be the underlying cause; a script can reduce immediate handling while preserving a fragile dependency. This is a hypothetical example with no project history or measured time savings.

Look for removable demand

For each candidate, record triggers, frequency, inputs, failure consequences and who uses the result. Stopping an unused report may be better than automating it. A repeated approval may still contain necessary judgment. Human involvement is not inherently a defect.

One-time rule cleanup, interface improvement or data-model repair can take more initial effort while creating enduring value. Small, stable repetitive tasks can also suit simple automation. No universal return formula is offered; absent actual workload and maintenance data, invented percentages do not justify a choice.

Include failure handling in the comparison. Automation may save routine effort while creating repeated manual repairs whenever input changes. Record exception, escalation and recovery work instead of counting only successful runs. Explicit human judgment may remain appropriate for consequential actions.

Apply the same scrutiny to AI

Agents and models handling unstructured input still need input-trust boundaries, action permissions, validation and failure handoff. Complete-looking model output does not establish a changed business state, and a tool’s success message still needs outcome verification. Choose a specific AI contribution, such as classification assistance, rather than converting every repeated task into conversation.

Define maintenance ownership, permissions, stopping conditions and recovery into a recognizable state. Otherwise the automation becomes another operational system and creates hidden work. Its code also needs versioning, review and validation.

A proposed evaluation starts with a bounded candidate, records existing workload and error types, then checks normal and failure paths after automation. Only actual observations support a workload-reduction claim. This article performed no such evaluation and promises no team efficiency improvement. Future evidence should preserve measurement definitions and observation periods.

Verifying postmortem actions addresses whether an improvement actually changes the problem. Compare repetition, necessity, enduring value and failure burden before selecting automation work.
