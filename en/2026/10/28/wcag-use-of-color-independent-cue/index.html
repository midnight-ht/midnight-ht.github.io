Without red and green, which orders failed?

Distinguish status meaning, sufficient lightness differences, and particular-color recognition in order tables.

M Midnight Engineering notes on development, quality, security, delivery, and operations. View Author Profile
Updated 2026-10-28 · Estimated read 11 min
Translations
zh-CN en

Article

Key takeaways

  • Identify the task performed by color.
  • Sufficient lightness differences can provide another visual distinction.
  • Screen-reader-only status does not supply a visible alternative.

An order list uses red dots for failures and green dots for completion, while every text label says “Processed.” A reader unable to distinguish the dot colors cannot identify which orders need attention. Determine what information color carries, then provide a usable visual distinction independent of identifying those hues.

Which dependency needs to change?

WCAG 2.2 SC1.4.1, a Level A criterion, prohibits color as the sole visual means of conveying information, indicating actions, prompting responses, or distinguishing elements. It does not prohibit colored status displays or require monochrome pages. Ask what visual evidence remains for the actual identification task.

The understanding document also explains lightness. Colors differing in hue and sufficiently in lightness can provide an additional visual distinction when their contrast is at least 3:1. Light green and dark red can meet the relevant distinction through lightness. Text and shape are therefore not the only possible additional cues, and using color does not invariably require adding words.

However, when meaning requires accurate recognition of a particular color, another indicator remains necessary regardless of that ratio. Knowing whether a border is green for valid or red for invalid is the document's example. Distinguishing two objects by lightness is different from knowing that a particular hue means failure. State whether the task is distinguishing objects, identifying status, or matching a series to a specifically colored legend.

How should an order table express status?

Consider an unexecuted batch-order page with completed, failed, and processing states. All currently use colored dots beside “Processed.” One repair is to show “Completed,” “Failed—retry available,” and “Processing” in the relevant rows, retaining color for scanning. This is a concrete design option rather than universal mandated wording.

Words must match real state. An accepted job is not necessarily complete, and an unretryable failure should not promise a retry action. The hypothetical business state machine must be defined separately. A color review can expose missing visual meaning without establishing that the underlying status data are correct.

Where space is limited, distinguishable shapes and understandable explanations may work. Check each row rather than relying on tiny, nearly identical symbols and a distant legend. A legend lost during scrolling or unavailable alongside magnified content can impede the task. Short status words are a direct choice here, not a normative requirement for every table.

Charts offer other options. Different line styles can distinguish trends when their names and identifiers remain clearly associated. Every point need not carry a sentence. Check crossings, zoom, and the actual lines: a dashed legend sample does not help if both plotted series remain solid.

Why is grayscale insufficient as an acceptance result?

Grayscale can reveal similar lightness, but it neither reproduces every visual condition nor verifies business meaning. Even if two dots remain distinguishable, ask whether the reader knows which means failure. If interpretation still requires knowing their original hues, the task relies on particular-color recognition. Keep the diagnostic tool's output separate from the information-design conclusion.

A proposed test starts by finding a failed order without relying on color names, opening its details, and checking permitted actions. Include processing-to-failed and failed-to-completed updates, plus an empty list. Text and graphics should update for the same object; changing a dot while leaving stale completion text is an incomplete repair.

For a lightness-based distinction, retain actual colors, luminance calculations, ratio, and the task the difference supports. Transparency, backgrounds, and themes can alter the rendered combination. No color measurements or order-page tests were executed here. These are proposed checks rather than a conformance report.

Text contrast, necessary graphic contrast, and sole reliance on color require separate judgments. The related non-text contrast article examines necessary visual information against adjacent colors. One passing ratio cannot approve the whole page or establish correct state meaning.

Is screen-reader-only status enough?

It does not establish that this visual problem has been addressed. A sighted reader with difficulty distinguishing colors may use no screen reader. Hidden “Failed” text supplies no alternative visible evidence. Provide the appropriate visual distinction and examine programmatic information separately.

Adding a hidden name to a red dot while keeping visible “Processed” leaves the visual ambiguity. Conversely, clear visible wording does not establish correct reading order, accessible names, or update announcements. Record visible presentation, computed accessibility information, and change behavior independently.

Retain a status mapping: what must be identified, which cue supports it, and how that cue changes. A reader should be able to find orders needing attention without first guessing a hue. Whether a particular page achieves that still depends on its actual content, colors, and interaction results.
