Tie Each Form Answer to a Service Decision

A field protocol names who uses an answer, which decision it changes, which users need to provide it, and how it is validated, retained, and deleted. A clear label is not a reason to collect data.

M Midnight Engineering notes on development, quality, security, delivery, and operations. View Author Profile
Updated 2026-11-24 · Estimated read 30 min
Translations
zh-CN en

Article

Key takeaways

  • Connect each field's purpose to a decision, user branch, consumer, and lifecycle; if no one can name its use, validate the purpose before making it required.
  • Whether a label is understandable, whether the service needs the answer, and whether it should be retained or reused are separate reviews.
  • Test not-applicable, declined, stale, and collection-failure states across APIs, storage, permissions, and deletion; unknown must not default to no.

Before a form field goes into production, a team should be able to answer four questions: who will use the answer, which service decision it changes, which users need to provide it and when, and who handles it when it becomes stale. Without that chain, “it might be useful later” is not a sufficient collection rationale. That record should come before the interface copy: it gives product, design, and engineering a shared account of the data’s purpose rather than merely a shared input control.

A protocol here is neither a legal document nor a privacy notice. It is a service-design and engineering record describing the path from a question to an operational action. A comprehensible label is an interface requirement. Explaining why the organization needs the answer and how it handles it is a separate data-governance and service-design requirement. The first does not prove the second.

Do not use one checkbox to answer three different questions

Start by separating three reviews.

First, can people tell what to enter? Labels, format examples, required-field cues, and error messages should help users provide a suitable answer. W3C’s Understanding document for WCAG 2.2 Success Criterion 3.3.2 says labels or instructions should be provided for fields that require user input. The same page warns that too much instruction can make task completion harder. This standard concerns whether people can understand an input; it does not decide whether a service has a reason to collect the data.

Second, does the service need the answer? Identify a specific eligibility check, routing, notification, payment, fulfilment, or follow-up action. A blank answer exposes the rationale: when no service step changes, the field probably has not earned a place in the form. Conversely, genuinely necessary information does not have to be requested from every user at every stage.

Third, how long should the answer be retained, and who may reuse it? An answer may serve a purpose at submission time without justifying indefinite retention or access by every downstream team. Record access roles, correction paths, a retention period or deletion trigger, and who reassesses a proposed new purpose. A new analytics subscription should not silently change the data’s use.

GOV.UK’s guide to structuring forms calls this advance list a question protocol. It recommends recording why information is needed, how it will be used, which users need to provide it, how accuracy will be checked, and how the information will be kept up to date and secure. The guide recommends designing for common scenarios and using branches so irrelevant users do not have to answer. The page says it was first published on 7 December 2016 and last updated on 7 August 2018. This article borrows its design method; it does not present the guide as new legislation. The UK Information Commissioner’s Office (ICO) describes the UK GDPR data-minimisation principle as requiring personal data to be adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary for a specified purpose. The ICO page also says that guidance is under review following changes introduced by the Data (Use and Access) Act. That legal context should therefore be checked by an appropriate privacy lead in the organizations and jurisdictions where it applies. This product-design article does not establish a legal conclusion for every region.

Write each field as a traceable data contract

A team may keep this agreement as a table, schema metadata, or a requirements section; what matters is that others can find it. Other people must be able to find the answer. For each field, record at least: a stable identifier and the user-facing question; the service purpose; the roles or systems that read it; the decision it changes; applicable branches and timing; expected format and validation; whether its source is user input or a system-derived value; storage location, access scope, and retention rule; how the user can correct it; what happens when it is absent or untrusted; and who reviews a future use.

Consider a hypothetical venue-booking service where people can request a public meeting room and some applicants may need an accessibility arrangement. Where an answer to “Do you need an accessibility arrangement?” changes room assignment, the service could ask for booking type first and show the follow-up only to relevant applicants. For the venue example, the agreement covers more than a boolean field: it would say when the question appears, which scheduling role receives the result, how the user can update it, and whether the answer belongs in ordinary event analytics. No real booking system was connected and no user research was conducted for this hypothetical example.

By contrast, a team may insist that “department” be required because it could be useful for future segmentation. If no eligibility rule, notification route, or fulfilment step currently reads the answer, ask whether that future analysis is an approved service purpose or just an unowned possibility. If the service only needs to distinguish application types, a narrower question in the relevant flow—or a derivation from existing service context—may avoid collecting the same information again. Do not make every user fill in a value nobody is responsible for keeping accurate just to make the database or organizational chart look complete.

The contract should also explain how the answer relates to later states. A mailing address changes. One submission proves only that a user supplied a value at a particular time; it cannot guarantee the value remains accurate. If the service uses it to make a delivery decision months later, the protocol should say when to ask for confirmation, how a correction works, and how an obsolete address is removed from the dispatch flow. The same applies to dates of birth, organizations, and contact details: format validation checks a string, not whether real-world information stays current.

Carry the branch condition into the API and data model

A protocol cannot live only in research notes. Implementation can connect field metadata to service rules: which user branches display the question, which workflow states require it, who can read it, whether it can be edited, and which state triggers deletion or anonymization. The client can control presentation and guidance, but the server must recheck applicability; it cannot trust a browser’s required attribute or hidden-field state. OWASP’s Input Validation Cheat Sheet likewise advises server-side validation even when the browser checks the same fields, because client-only checks are bypassable. That supports an input-boundary check, not the separate justification for collecting a field. The data model should distinguish states such as “not asked yet,” “not applicable,” “user declined,” and “unknown” rather than collapsing them into an empty string or default false.

That distinction affects service behavior. Suppose an applicant answers “I do not need a special arrangement.” That is an explicit negative answer. A user who never reached the question’s branch is “not applicable.” A failed request is “unknown.” If all three are stored as false, a downstream service may assign someone to a standard room even though no question was asked. First decide which states the service must distinguish. Then choose among nullable values, an enumerated status, separate collection-time and source fields, or a separate record. Do not add state complexity for theoretical completeness when no consumer needs the distinction.

User-provided and system-derived values should also remain distinguishable. A system may derive an initial scheduling group from a booking type. If a coordinator can reassign it based on the applicant’s circumstances, the derived value should not masquerade as a fact the user supplied. Recording source, generation time, rule version, and human edits helps identify whether an error came from user input, a changed rule, or a stale cache. For sensitive or eligibility-affecting answers, limit logging too: copying raw personal data into long-lived access logs for debugging can bypass the field’s intended lifecycle. OWASP’s Logging Cheat Sheet lists sensitive personal data and some personally identifiable information among data that should usually not be recorded directly. Ask whether troubleshooting needs the value itself or only the question version, branch, and failure category.

When a field’s purpose changes, engineering should trigger a review instead of adding a database column and waiting for consumers to discover it. A versioned protocol can name an owner; changes to API schemas, consumer inventories, permission tests, and deletion jobs can be reviewed in the same change record. The transition plan should state how old values migrate, whether existing users must be informed or reconfirm, and how to roll back if the migration fails. Our article on research consent and data lineage discusses the boundary of tracking information after it leaves its original context. This article focuses on the earlier entry point: why the question exists and which service path receives its answer.

Choose whether to ask now, later, not at all, or derive

A valid purpose does not always require collecting the answer on the first screen. First ask whether the answer changes eligibility or determines whether the user can continue. If it does, asking early may prevent someone from completing a long form only to discover they are ineligible. An answer useful only after someone opts into an optional service can usually be requested at that branch. If an operator can obtain the value from a reliable service record, asking again may add errors and maintenance cost—but the team must not assume it may reuse data collected for a different purpose.

Then examine the cost of missing information. If refusing to answer blocks a material part of the service, document which rule requires the answer and who approved that rule; a NOT NULL database constraint is not a business rationale. For an answer that only improves later communication, an optional question may fit if the form clearly explains what skipping it means. “Optional” is not a universal magic word: optional answers can still be collected, stored, and used in downstream decisions.

Finally, test whether an alternative is more reliable. Derivation can reduce entry burden but inherits stale or incorrect source data. Free text accommodates exceptions but is harder to validate, classify, and restrict. A drop-down can simplify routing but may force people into the wrong category if it has no “other” or correction path. Choose based on the actual decision, user comprehension, ability to maintain the value, access controls, and the cost of correcting errors—not development hours alone.

If a field is useful only in a later optional step, should it be required at the start?

Usually not. Define the service branch and decision that trigger the field, then ask when the user reaches that branch. Asking earlier may be justified if the answer determines eligibility or the service genuinely needs to prepare the later path in advance. This is a product judgment based on GOV.UK’s guidance on branching forms, not a universal legal rule. If the question serves another purpose or a regulatory obligation, reassess that purpose and its conditions instead of using the branch as a reason to collect more data.

Test whether the protocol still matches actual behavior

Testing should go beyond “an empty required field returns an error.” Build a branch matrix: eligible users see the question and understand its format; ineligible users are not asked to invent an answer; users can skip optional questions and continue as promised; a timed-out request becomes unknown rather than defaulting to no; stale values prompt correction or stop influencing a decision; downstream consumers receive a corrected value; deletion triggers are respected by the database, search index, cache, and export jobs within their respective boundaries; and unauthorized roles cannot read sensitive answers. Map every test to a purpose, state, consumer, or lifecycle rule in the protocol.

Also test mismatches such as a field that looks optional in the interface but is rejected by the server. API contract tests should cover a browser that omits the field, a missing server answer, an old client sending a retired option, and a downstream message delivered more than once. An event record might capture the question version, branch, answer status, and resulting decision, but logging the raw answer requires a separate rationale. Many quality issues need only the rule version that routed the user, not a duplicate of their personal information in analytics logs.

Accessibility and business necessity need separate acceptance checks. W3C’s WCAG 2.2 explanation says labels or instructions should help people understand a field’s purpose and format, while warning that unnecessary information can create confusion. A further note explains that visible labels alone do not automatically satisfy separate criteria about semantic association between a label and its control. Engineering checks can verify that labels are programmatically associated with controls, error messages explain how to fix the problem, and keyboard and assistive technology users receive the same question and state. The product review asks whether the answer is actually used in the stated decision. Passing an automated accessibility check does not prove that a field is necessary; conversely, a justified question may still be hard to answer because of poor labels, branching, or error handling.

Without user research or production data, teams can still do a tabletop review: follow a fictional application from the question through routing, handler, storage, access, and deletion, and check that every step has an owner. Then simulate not-applicable, declined, stale, and collection-failure states. This is a proposed verification plan, not a test already executed, and it does not claim that removing fields improves conversion. If user testing follows, record the task, participant group, observation criteria, and failure cases. If post-release behavior is analyzed, define the denominator, observation window, and handling of missing data; do not attribute a change in completion rate to one field without evidence.

The value of a field protocol is that an answer keeps its origin and boundaries through requirements, interface, API, storage, and operations. The next time someone says “let’s add one more field,” ask which decision changes, who it applies to, when the answer is used, who maintains it, and how the service behaves without it. If the team cannot answer, keep the field on a list of assumptions to validate instead of turning that guess into data every user must provide.
