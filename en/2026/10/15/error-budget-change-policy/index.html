Which Changes Should Stop When the Error Budget Is Exhausted?

An error budget is a written contract between measurement and change decisions. Verify scope and causes, restrict higher-risk actions, and define exceptions, approvers, and evidence for lifting restrictions.

Article

Key takeaways

  • Budget exhaustion triggers a decision constraint; it is not an automatic freeze detached from user impact and measurement scope.
  • Separate risk-increasing releases, risk-reducing recovery actions, and time-sensitive security fixes; give exceptions approvers and rollback evidence.
  • When collection is delayed, mark the budget unknown and use a pre-agreed provisional state and review point rather than treating missing samples as zero consumption.

A reviewable default is to restrict production changes that add risk to the affected path without helping stop that failure when an error budget is exhausted. “Code freeze” is too blunt to serve as a complete policy. Teams should agree in advance on the service and user scope, budget window, restricted change classes, exception approvals, and recovery criteria. Otherwise, when the budget is nearly gone, a dashboard can report a number but cannot tell the on-call engineer what to stop.

Consider a four-week budget that has just crossed its threshold while the team is preparing a feature release. Most of the recent consumption came from a company-wide network failure. Three different questions now matter: Were the SLO’s target users affected? Did the measurement rule include those requests? Would the proposed release increase or reduce current risk on the affected path? Budget balance and risk are related, but they are not the same quantity. Collapsing them into “freeze or not” makes the policy either over-restrictive or so exception-prone that it has no force.

The budget does not decide by itself

The budget usually derives from an agreed SLO: the tolerated proportion of missed service objectives over a defined period. It is not, by itself, a measure of incident severity, root-cause probability, or change risk. Budget consumption can trigger discussion and constrain decisions, but the balance alone cannot establish that every change must stop. Google’s Service Level Objectives chapter also cautions against treating 100% reliability as a universal goal and says SLO choices carry product and business tradeoffs.

One example policy in the Google SRE Workbook is specific about its scope. If the service exceeds its budget over the preceding four weeks, it halts changes and releases except for P0 issues and security fixes. It also describes cases where non-reliability features may continue, such as a company-wide network problem, out-of-scope users, or misclassified errors. Google published this as an example policy in 2018; it is not a universal rule. What is useful is that the threshold, restricted actions, and exceptions appear together in a policy approved by the relevant roles, rather than becoming a negotiation improvised during an incident.

In its Implementing SLOs chapter, the Workbook says the written policy should specify what happens when the budget is exhausted, who acts, and the service context, calculation, rationale, and escalation path. An error-budget policy is therefore both a measurement definition and a decision contract among product, development, and SRE. If participants have not agreed to enforce it, the SLO remains a reporting metric rather than a dependable control.

Find out why the balance changed

Before restricting changes, verify what the indicator measures. A useful checklist asks: Which user requests form the SLI denominator? What counts as success? Is the window a rolling four weeks or a fixed quarter? How are requests assigned to windows? Can retries or late events be counted twice? How are load tests, internal traffic, and users outside the service promise handled? When data is delayed, is the dashboard showing a finalized value or an estimate?

Suppose a service promises 99.9% success for eligible requests over four weeks. With one million eligible requests in that window, the error budget is 1,000 unsuccessful requests. A dashboard shows 1,080. This hypothetical calculation illustrates that 80 events exceed the budget; it is not site traffic. If 200 events came from load tests that the established SLI and policy exclude, a traceable reclassification could change the result. But changing the denominator only after seeing an overrun would make the decision unauditable. Preserve the original events, reason for correction, effective boundary, and approver. A one-time metric adjustment is not a way to restore reliability.

Root-cause ownership and user impact are also different dimensions. A shared DNS or network failure may not have been caused by the team, yet it can still make target users’ requests fail. If the SLO promises to cover those requests, the budget should reflect the service result users experienced. Whether the team may continue non-reliability work can be decided by an agreed policy. That keeps responsibility attribution from masquerading as a measurement fact, while avoiding the assumption that a shared infrastructure fault should trigger identical actions for every team.

When defining an SLO from user outcomes, clarify success, the user population, and boundaries such as asynchronous task completion. If definitions change midway through an incident, old and new windows may no longer be comparable. Release restrictions should refer to a versioned SLI rule, not a dashboard label changed on the fly.

Classify risky actions, not just code and non-code

A policy that only says “stop code releases” leaves the same risk path open through configuration, database migrations, traffic shifts, or infrastructure templates. Conversely, rolling back a release linked to rising errors, disabling a newly enabled path, or fixing an actively exploited security issue may reduce current risk. The direction of risk depends on the affected path, blast radius, and recovery options, not the name of the change type.

One practical policy can distinguish three classes. First, changes that add risk without addressing the current reliability issue: for example, expanding unvalidated traffic, adding a new hard dependency, or performing an irreversible data migration. When the budget is exceeded, pause these or require stronger approval. Second, recovery actions that reduce current risk: rolling back a version associated with an error increase, disabling a failing dependency, or adding capacity through a validated procedure. Record the operator, version, observation metrics, and rollback path, but do not ban the action merely because the policy contains the word “freeze.” Third, time-sensitive security, compliance, or user-data protection fixes. Specify who approves them, the minimum checks required, and how to use a reversible, limited rollout if a full validation cycle cannot be completed in time.

Intent alone is not enough. Calling a change “reliability work” does not prove it reduces risk; a database rebuild may fix corruption or introduce locks and data loss. Before approval, the change record should answer four questions: Which user path changes? What evidence connects it to the incident or budget burn? How will the blast radius expand in stages? Which observation triggers a stop or rollback? If those answers are missing, the exception has not been justified merely by an urgent label.

The risk chapter goes beyond an all-or-nothing freeze; its discussion also mentions slowing releases or reducing push velocity when the budget approaches exhaustion. For a high-risk change to a shared database, a full pause may be appropriate; a copy edit in an isolated service may warrant a different approval threshold. This is an editorial application of the principle, not a fixed Google classification scheme for every system.

Give exceptions an owner, expiry, and end condition

Exceptions often hollow out a policy because “urgent” has no definition or expiration. Each exception should record the related incident or security issue, users protected and problem addressed, version and scope, approver, checks not performed, observation points, stop/rollback threshold, and review time. Complete the record before acting when possible. If the situation does not allow that, fill it in promptly afterward and explain why. Do not rewrite missing evidence as a passed check.

Imagine that the budget is exhausted and a new release is causing login failures. A policy that prohibits every deployment could block rollback. A policy that permits any change “related to the incident” could let unrelated features slip through. A reviewable approach limits rollback to a known version, bounded traffic, and a named owner; observe the recovery indicators before expanding the action. This is a policy exercise, not a real service event. No freeze, rollback, or security fix was executed for this article, and no reduction in failures was measured.

The escalation path should also work when restrictions fail to improve service. If the budget calculation is disputed, the SLI owner can explain event scope and calculation; the policy approver can then determine whether the original agreement applies. If the SLO itself does not represent user experience, revise it through a documented process rather than silently changing the formula during an incident. The example policy escalates unresolved disputes to its CTO; another organization may assign a different role, but that person must be findable and the decision’s scope and window must be recorded.

Decide when to lift restrictions and how to test the policy

Write the exit criteria before a freeze begins. They may include more than “the budget resets next month”: the user-facing failure has stopped; rollback or mitigation has been observed on the affected path; monitoring gaps and misclassification have been fixed; recovery work has an owner; and untouched changes have been reclassified. A fixed-window reset does not prove the cause is gone. Conversely, a low budget does not mean every risk-reducing action must wait for the window to end.

A tabletop exercise can test the policy without creating a production incident. Prepare cases with explicit inputs: a code regression consumes the budget; a shared network outage affects users; test traffic is counted incorrectly; a security fix cannot wait for the ordinary release window; or rollback itself would trigger a data migration. Ask on-call, development, product, and SRE participants to write down their action, approver, and required evidence. Check whether they reach a consistent decision from the same events. The test is whether the policy can be executed, whether classifications can be reviewed, and whether exceptions have boundaries—not whether the team produces a flattering pass rate.

Include failure paths. The incident may be mitigated while the SLI backfill is delayed, leaving the dashboard in an exhausted state. Or the balance may appear to recover because the collection system stopped recording samples. The policy should say which secondary source to consult, who can declare a provisional state, whether restrictions continue, and when to review. Without fallback evidence, report “budget unknown”; do not treat missing data as zero consumption.

Should a delayed dashboard trigger the usual freeze?

Do not interpret “no samples received yet” as “no failures,” and do not overwrite settled data with a temporary formula just to make the balance look healthy. Teams can define a provisional state in advance: pause new, irreversible, high-risk expansion on the affected path; continue reversible actions that reduce immediate harm; have the SLI owner inspect raw events or fallback telemetry; and set a short review time. This temporary restriction is a risk-control proposal, not a fixed step prescribed by the Google example policy above.

If fallback data confirms the overrun, apply the normal restriction classes and exception process. If collection is broken and user impact cannot be estimated, label the budget unknown and identify who decides short-term risk, which proxy signals they use, and when the decision will be revisited. Unknown is not the same as safe, but it need not automatically freeze the entire system. The provisional state must not continue indefinitely: if evidence is still unavailable at the review point, escalate to the policy approver and record why restrictions continue or the plan changes.

Evaluate the policy by more than freeze duration or release count. Over a defined period, review whether budget-burning events can be traced to change records, whether restrictions applied to affected user paths, whether exceptions were reviewed on time, and whether the same outage class returned. These are candidate evaluation dimensions, not results measured for this article. It also calls for a postmortem after an incident consumes a specified share of the budget and places severe recurring problems into planning; teams still need to choose thresholds that fit their service and capacity.

After an error budget is exhausted, the team needs more than “stop everything.” It needs a policy that connects measurement, risk-bearing actions, owners, and recovery evidence. Verify the SLI and user scope first; distinguish changes that add risk from recovery actions and time-sensitive security work; give exceptions an approver, expiry, and rollback condition; then test disputed cases in a tabletop exercise. If the data cannot explain why the budget changed, mark the balance unknown and repair measurement rather than changing the definition to reopen releases.
