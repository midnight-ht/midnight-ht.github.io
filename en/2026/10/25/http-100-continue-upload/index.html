Can Expect: 100-continue reject a large upload before its body?

Inspect early rejection, client waiting, 417 fallback, and proxy-generated 100 responses through actual timing.

M Midnight Engineering notes on development, quality, security, delivery, and operations. View Author Profile
Updated 2026-10-25 · Estimated read 13 min
Translations
zh-CN en

Article

Key takeaways

  • 100 is interim; continuation still needs a final outcome.
  • Clients need not wait a fixed period before sending the body.
  • Distinguish proxy-generated 100 from origin approval using both connection legs.

Expect: 100-continue gives a server an opportunity to reject a request before a large body is sent. It does not guarantee that the client always waits for permission. It is useful when the method, target, and headers can reveal errors early. To establish that unwanted transfer was avoided, inspect actual header, body, and response timing rather than the final status alone.

What does 100 Continue permit?

RFC 9110's Expect definition describes a client preparing to send content and requesting an interim response when the method, target, and headers do not already justify a final response. Under the stated HTTP/1.1-or-later conditions, the origin must immediately send a final response determinable from those inputs or send 100 to encourage transmission. It must not wait for the body before sending 100. A server may omit 100 if some or all content has already arrived, or if framing indicates no content; absence of 100 alone therefore does not establish a protocol failure.

100's semantics mean the initial request has been received and not yet rejected. They do not mean that upload, storage, or business approval has completed. Content format, integrity, and business checks may still fail. A premature connection closure can also leave the final result unavailable; the interim response cannot replace it.

Ask which decisions genuinely require only headers. Unsupported methods, disallowed targets, or some authentication failures can be determined without receiving the entire content. A check depending on file contents still needs the body. Negotiation cannot make the server know those contents in advance. A content-free request must not generate a 100-continue expectation.

How would a large upload complete?

Suppose a client uploads a hypothetical 200 MB document through a proxy to a controlled resource. The size is illustrative; no upload or savings measurement was performed. The client sends its method, target, necessary headers, and expectation, then initially waits. If the origin can determine invalid credentials from those headers, it sends a final refusal. The client should handle that refusal according to its actual library behavior, rather than interpreting it as an invitation to send the body.

On a successful candidate path, the origin first sends 100, the client transmits content, and the origin performs its remaining checks before a final success response. The interface then obtains or presents the confirmed result. On a failure path, 100 is followed by content transmission, format rejection, and a final error. Both demonstrate that the result remains undecided after 100; the interface cannot mark the task complete at that point.

Also inspect connection handling after early refusal. A server returning a final status before reading all content should indicate whether it intends to close or continue reading. Inconsistent handling of remaining content across client, proxy, and application can affect subsequent connection use. Framing and reuse need version-specific verification; semantic analysis does not certify an implementation's connection safety.

A counterexample is a client sending headers and body together, followed by a server-generated 100, while monitoring declares that permission was obtained before transfer. Some cost has already occurred. Another is a capable origin behind an entry proxy that buffers the complete body before forwarding headers. Early origin refusal would not establish early client-side avoidance.

May the client send content when its wait expires?

Yes. The specification requires no fixed waiting period and permits transmission without receiving a response; waiting should not be indefinite. An Expect header therefore does not establish that no body bytes can leave early. Inspect the client library and configuration for its wait limit, transmission trigger, and handling of a final refusal.

For 417 in response to a 100-continue expectation, the specification recommends repeating the request without that expectation because the chain may not support it. Distinguish this from a business rejection: 417 concerns an unmet expectation, not specifically a file-content review failure. Applications still need to assess replay safety, body availability, and current client state instead of converting every failure into another upload.

A one-use data source may not supply a complete second body. Establish whether content comes from a rereadable file, resumable stream, or one-shot source, then choose reopening, buffering, or an explicit failure. These are implementation decisions; the RFC does not manufacture a replayable source. No library's automatic fallback was verified here.

Does a proxy's 100 establish origin approval?

No. For a qualifying request, the proxy must either send an immediately determinable final response or forward the request line and headers toward the origin. If configuration or prior interaction suggests that the next server supports only HTTP/1.0, it may generate 100 itself. The interim response received by the client therefore does not prove the origin has completed every header check.

Observe both client-to-entry and entry-to-origin legs: protocol version, header transmission, first body data, interim and final responses, and connection closure. Where the entry buffers content, origin receipt time is not the start of public-network transfer. Final status alone cannot identify which participant first encouraged sending. Keep the necessary timing and correlation while avoiding credential and sensitive-file capture.

Choose the mechanism by balancing opportunities for early refusal, body cost, additional waiting, and chain compatibility. Small requests may not justify waiting. Large bodies with errors often detectable in headers warrant verification using the actual client and proxy combination. Protocol availability alone establishes no percentage improvement or bandwidth saving.

The site's 202 and terminal-state article examines completion after acceptance. This article concerns negotiation before body transmission. First establish that early refusal really precedes transmission, then verify that both successful and unsuccessful continuation reach a final outcome. Without those observations, an installed header is the only demonstrated fact, not effective early protection of the upload.
