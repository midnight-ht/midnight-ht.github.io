Should incident severity follow alert counts or user impact?

Replace alert counts with an illustrative decision record, covering uncertainty, coordination, and residual work after apparent recovery.

M Midnight Engineering notes on development, quality, security, delivery, and operations. View Author Profile
Updated 2026-10-31 · Estimated read 13 min
Translations
zh-CN en

Article

Key takeaways

  • Alert counts describe symptoms; severity should connect impact and response.
  • Preserve evidence, ownership, and a review point for provisional decisions.
  • De-escalation needs reduced-impact evidence and residual ownership.

Incident severity should connect user impact to the response that must be organized. Alert counts describe symptoms. One failure can generate many repeated alerts, while one quiet signal can indicate that a critical task is impossible. Classification should mobilize the appropriate people and resources rather than reward the loudest component.

Severity should guide action

PagerDuty's Severity Levels handbook gives organization-specific examples, advises using the higher level when uncertain, and permits coordinated response for lower-severity issues. These practices are not a universal standard that every team must copy. The source was checked again on October 31, 2026. No real incident or exercise was conducted.

Define affected user capabilities, available alternatives, potential expansion, and confidence in the evidence. A list of component names is insufficient because the same component can have different consequences across product journeys. User count alone is also incomplete when exposure of data or irreversible actions changes the nature of the risk.

Connect levels to real actions: coordination ownership, authorization for riskier recovery changes, updates to affected users, and reassessment. A policy that names severities without providing response roles still leaves people debating numbers while recovery waits.

Replace alert counting with a complete decision record

Consider an unexecuted scenario without real customer counts, losses, or universal thresholds. At 10:00 a test product produces many thumbnail-failure alerts. Original images remain available. At the same time, one payment-callback signal suggests that some orders may not complete confirmation. The second outcome is uncertain; the larger thumbnail alert count cannot establish the greater impact.

A tabletop record could continue as follows. At 10:02, the on-call responder records that image browsing remains usable and payment confirmation is unknown. At 10:04, an authorized test account fails the post-payment confirmation task and the relevant task-to-interface evidence is retained. At 10:05, the response owner assigns a provisional level under the organization's existing policy and brings in payment and order maintainers. The next impact review is set for 10:10. Every time is illustrative.

The example does not preassign a highest or lowest severity. It identifies what is known, what remains unknown, who decides, what begins immediately, and when reassessment is required. Reliable evidence of a harmless display delay could justify adjustment; persistent order inconsistency could justify escalation.

Preserve the provisional assessment when later evidence arrives. Its original basis and revision time help distinguish reasonable uncertainty from neglected information. A retrospective has more data than the responder had. Evaluate information flow and decisions rather than pretending the final classification was available from the beginning.

Who can request escalation before impact is fully known?

The organization should authorize the relevant roles in advance. A responder should not need a complete loss calculation before requesting help. A useful proposal records the provisional level, triggering evidence, missing information, reassessment owner, and next checkpoint. This is a suggested record structure, not authority to set another team's escalation rules.

A stronger response has costs: additional coordination, riskier changes, and communication obligations. Conservative escalation therefore still needs continued judgment. Collect evidence on the critical user task and adjust under policy when the scope becomes clearer, rather than maintaining an initial classification until everyone disengages.

Coordination needs and severity are related but separate. A smaller issue may cross several teams; a larger issue still needs clear leadership to prevent conflicting changes. Incident command and handoff responsibility addresses those roles. Here, the question is how impact assessment should drive their activation.

Why is alert recovery insufficient for de-escalation?

Suppose callback errors recover while previously affected orders remain unresolved. New requests succeeding does not establish that the earlier backlog is complete. Review backlog, state consistency, compensation ownership, and user outcomes before explaining which impacts ended and which responsibilities remain.

The opposite is possible too: an old alert remains active during a holding window while the critical task works again. Alert state alone does not establish unchanged impact. Record both technical signals and task results, then apply current evidence rather than rewriting the impact description to match a dashboard color.

Escalation and de-escalation consequently emphasize different evidence. An unresolved critical risk can motivate escalation. De-escalation should explain why the earlier impact has reduced, who owns residual work, and whether coordination remains necessary. That is an editorial recommendation derived from an impact-first approach, not a universal quantitative formula from the handbook.

How can a team check whether its policy is usable?

A proposed tabletop exercise covers many low-impact alerts, one critical-task failure, uncertain impact, a lower-severity issue requiring coordination, and residual backlog after alert recovery. Record the decision owner, available evidence, actions, and review point. The goal is whether policy guides action, not whether everyone memorized the same number.

Persistent arguments about component rank suggest unclear impact definitions. Reluctance to request assistance suggests an authorization or escalation problem. Unowned residual work suggests missing completion criteria. Turn exercise findings into verifiable actions; verifying postmortem improvements discusses that subsequent responsibility.

These exercises have not been performed and no response-time improvement is reported. Before adoption, a service owner should map the record to existing on-call arrangements, user commitments, and risk requirements. An explanation of impact, action, and open responsibilities matters more than the number of levels in a table.
