Control referrer exposure separately from transport encryption

Review referrer disclosure by origin relationship and keep transport and authorization distinct.

M Midnight Engineering notes on development, quality, security, delivery, and operations. View Author Profile
Updated 2026-10-17 · Estimated read 6 min
Translations
zh-CN en

Article

HTTPS does not automatically hide referrer information deliberately sent with navigation or resource requests. Check Referrer-Policy across same-origin, cross-origin and downgrade paths. Transport encryption and the information a browser chooses to send are separate concerns, so paths and query values still need careful design.

Defaults retain some information

The MDN Referrer-Policy reference describes strict-origin-when-cross-origin as the default absent a valid policy. Same-origin requests include path and query information; cross-origin requests retain only the origin when security is maintained; less-secure destinations receive no referrer. Other policies differ. Inspect response and element settings rather than assuming every page uses the default.

Imagine a sensitive business identifier in a page query followed by a same-origin resource request. HTTPS alone does not establish that referrer information omits that path. This was not network-tested and does not mean every identifier is secret or every request sends a complete address. Origin relationship and policy conditions matter.

Referrer information is not authorization

Restricting the header does not remove secrets from the request's target URL or erase past logs. A navigation that includes a secret in its destination needs additional address, logging and data-flow review.

Nor is a plausible Referer an identity or permission proof. Its presence should not grant sensitive object access, and its absence does not automatically identify malicious traffic. Clients, user settings and policy affect disclosure; use proper authentication and authorization.

Legitimate analytics, defenses or integrations may use context, but define that need rather than defaulting to full-address disclosure. No policy is automatically suitable for every data flow. Evaluate actual information and consumers.

Observe real initiators

Proposed checks cover navigation, resource loading, same-origin and cross-origin destinations, and downgrades. Inspect effective page and element policies and what receivers observe. These tests were not executed and no browser configuration is claimed to be verified.

Use recognizable nonsensitive samples rather than sending real secrets to uncontrolled external destinations. Record the policy and origin relationship without publishing complete user addresses. Debugging artifacts need retention and disclosure controls too.

Policy changes can affect consumers of referrer context. Inventory and regression-check them. If a system uses referrer as its only authorization condition, repair that design rather than preserving disclosure solely for compatibility. A successful policy rollout does not eliminate all URL risks.

Evidence without secret disclosure covers another data boundary. Review the referrer header, target URL, encryption and authorization independently to state what the policy actually reduces.
